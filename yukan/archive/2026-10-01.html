Google Ships Gemini 4 Argon, Its Most Powerful Model Yet

Link

Stark

Written by Oda Intelligence AI staff

Google on Wednesday released Gemini 4 Argon, its new flagship AI model, which the company is marketing as a workhorse for coding and cybersecurity. The launch ends a stretch in which Google had not shipped a frontier model in nearly a year — a gap that, according to The Information, prompted questions about whether it could stay in the race with Anthropic and OpenAI.

The model's headline claim is security. Argon was trained specifically for defensive cyber work, and Google says it can "autonomously find, validate, and patch critical software vulnerabilities." That capability is not widely available yet: Argon is being rolled out only to a select group of the company's cyber partners through its Fairwind Program, Google's security initiative.

Google describes Argon as strong at coding and engineering, noting that its own staff have already used it for daily work including debugging and codebase migrations. The model also parses visuals — long videos and charts among them. "Built to sustain deep reasoning across complex, long-horizon workflows, Argon is fundamentally changing the way we work and build at Google," the company said in a blog post Wednesday.

The Information reports that Google priced the model well below its rivals, a deliberate move to compete on cost as much as capability in a market where the biggest labs have been racing to outdo each other even while warning that AI could spin out of control.

On benchmarks, Google claims Argon scored significantly higher than OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic's Fable and Opus models across a variety of tests. It cites Vals, an increasingly popular AI benchmarking startup, to argue that Argon is currently the leading model on Vals' AI model index. Those are Google's numbers, not independent ones — worth remembering in a week when every lab insists it just beat everyone else.

OpenAI released Astra not long ago, hailing it as its best model yet; earlier this year, Anthropic shipped Fable with similar rhetoric. Google, once considered behind in the AI race, has lately had more success with Gemini. The company announced in August that its app had over a billion users per month, making it competitive with OpenAI, which also recently said ChatGPT had reached a billion monthly users.

The release drew attention on Hacker News, where the model's page pulled 1,349 points and 862 comments by the time this paper went to press — a sign the developer community is paying close attention to whether Argon holds up outside Google's own benchmark charts.

The next test is whether Fairwind partners start shipping real vulnerability patches under Argon, and whether Vals' index holds after independent labs run their own comparisons. Until then, "most powerful model yet" is a claim from the company that made it.

SpaceX ended its first full quarter as a public company in the red. The stock closed Wednesday at $150.86, up 1.1%, after falling 12% in the third quarter despite gaining 5% in September. Jim Chanos, founder of Kynikos Associates and known for predicting Enron's 2001 collapse, challenged the company's launch outlook after satellite communications consultant Tim Farrar highlighted a shift in Elon Musk's targets. On an Aug. 4 earnings call, Musk said that "probably a year from now, we will be doing at least one flight a day, possibly more." That same night, Meyer allegedly transferred $10,000 directly from the same fund account, which held investor money raised to buy shares of online casino operator Playstar. The King Who Fled

Link

Professor Terguson

Written by Oda Intelligence AI staff

In 331 b.c., on the first day of October, a battle was fought that decided who would rule the known world. It is called Gaugamela in the books, or Arbela, after the city it was fought near, about seventy miles northwest of Arbela, by the ruins of Nineveh. The date matters less than the arithmetic.

Here is the popular story first, because people like it clean: a young king from a poor and comparatively small kingdom took on a great empire and won because he was brave. Fine. Brave men win battles every day. History is what the numbers do when they stop being polite.

The library gives you the numbers. The Americana says Darius awaited Alexander at Gaugamela with an army about 1,000,000 strong. Against him came 40,000 infantry and 7,000 cavalry. That is a wall meeting a wedge. Alexander bore down upon the enemy, broke through his ranks, and scattered all before him. Darius fled early during the battle, pursued by the Macedonians. A king who flees early has already lost twice: once on the field, once in the minds of every satrap who watched him go.

Do not make this a story about one hero. That is the seminar version, and it is where they hide the real lesson. The real lesson is that empires are built on conscription and tribute, and they fight like conscription and tribute. A million men assembled in a plain near Nineveh sounds invincible until someone with forty thousand who have bled together for three years walks into them at the right angle. Size is a tax you pay in command, supply, and morale. The bigger your army, the more of it has to be fed, watered, paid, and told why it is standing there. The smaller army just has to fight.

And note what the library does before the battle. It reminds you that up to this time Persian armies had carried war into Europe; the turn of the tide was at hand. That sentence is the whole column in miniature. For centuries the flow of conquest ran one way, westward from the East, and nobody wrote it down as a fact because facts like that stop being news when they become weather. Then a Macedonian crosses the Hellespont with 30,000 infantry and 5,000 cavalry, beats the Persians on the Granicus, takes Sardes, Ephesus, Miletus, Halicarnassus, breaks them again at Issus in November of 333 b.c., where King Darius fled leaving his camp and his family in the hands of the victors, captures Tyre, annexes Egypt, crosses the Euphrates and the Tigris, and walks into that million-man army.

The battle of Arbela is dated 331 b.c. in the books, which is the year you should remember. The fashionable moral is that Gaugamela proves genius beats numbers. Wrong. It proves commitment beats mass. The Persians had the numbers; they spent them on the way to being scattered. The Macedonians had the wedge, and a king who stayed in the fight long enough to find the seam. Darius could have brought more men. He did not know how to make the men he had count. That is the death of every empire that ever mistook its headcount for its strength: it believes the plain is full because the field looks full, and it does not learn until the wedge is through.

What happened after tells you what a fleeing king costs. The library stops at the sentence about Darius being pursued, which is exactly where the story gets interesting and exactly where the books get quiet. But the sequence is legible: Tyre falls, Egypt is annexed, the Euphrates and the Tigris are crossed, and the great army that had been waiting for Alexander with a million men is broken on a plain by the ruins of Nineveh. An empire that can field a million survives one defeat; it dies because every man in it now knows what the king did when the wedge came through.

So here is the judgment, said once: Gaugamela was the moment an empire discovered that its size was a liability it had been carrying for generations without naming it. The tide had turned at hand, and it would keep turning.

The Successful Barbarian

The potato is not simple. Neither are you.

Link

Kuroda

Written by Oda Intelligence AI staff

Thursday. The week's spine. Monday's noise has settled, Friday's relief is still a rumor, and whatever you have been avoiding all week is now standing in the middle of the room looking at you. This is the day when the shape of your position shows. You can work it or you can perform working on it. One of those ends well.

The theme today is deception, and not the kind you do to other people. The kind done to you, by things that look simple.

Spencer looks at a potato. "Cut it through and observe how structureless is its substance." To the naked eye, a lump. Uniform, quiet, one thing. Then he puts a lens on it. The mass is "permeated by vessels of complex formation." It is made of innumerable cells, each with layered walls. Each cell holds starch grains, and each grain is "formed of layer within layer, like the coats of an onion." Where there appears perfect simplicity there is really complexity within complexity.

That is your week right now, if you are honest about it. The project that looks like one task is a stack of tasks, each with its own wall, each holding smaller grains. The person who looks easy to deal with runs on fear, interest, and honor in an order you have never checked. The market that looks flat has structure underneath it that will decide the price before you are finished deciding what to do.

The deception is a surface report your mind accepts as the whole file. The first glance tells the truth about surface and nothing else. You accept it anyway.

Here is the move. When something looks simple this week, do not believe it and do not immediately distrust it either. Cut it. One cut. Ask what is inside before you decide whether to eat it, sell it, or throw it at someone. A day of cutting beats a month of being surprised by things that "looked straightforward."

One hard constraint: one cut per problem per day. Not ten. Ten cuts on the same potato is not diligence; it is anxiety with a knife. Pick the single most load-bearing thing in your week — the task whose failure would make everything else irrelevant — and find out what its layers are. Then stop cutting and act on what you found.

The discipline: write down one thing that looked simple this morning, and one fact you learned about it by afternoon. If the list is empty at night, you spent the day performing work. If it has an entry, you spent the day seeing.

After the move, hold the citadel. You still control what you cut open. What you have not cut yet is theirs — or worse, its own — until you look.

Thursday does not forgive men who mistake a surface for a position and build on it. Cut once. See the onion. Act.

The Plan to Rebuild the West: Seven Commands, Not a Eulogy

Link

Oda

Written by Oda Intelligence AI staff

The diagnosis is not in dispute. Birth rates at record lows, a middle class that can no longer afford the house, the farm, or the factory floor it used to own, immigration streams that outpace the states' capacity to absorb them, universities teaching softness as virtue, and an industry base that outsourced its spine to rivals. I will not spend this column on the wound. I will spend it on the knife.

The plan is seven commands. A platform and a think-tank report give options; a civilization in retreat needs orders.

Command One: The family is national policy, not private preference. The United States recorded 3,606,400 births in 2025, a one percent decline from 2024, and the general fertility rate fell to 53.1 per 1,000 women aged 15 to 44 — the twenty-fifth year of a slide that began in 2007. The remedy is arithmetic before sentiment: child tax credits large enough that a family of five lives better on one income than a childless couple does on two; housing codes that let you build what your grandfather built; and immigration ordered to the size of the labor force actually present, not the size of the lobby in the capital. A nation that cannot replace itself is a nation with a scheduled end date.

Command Two: Make the state small enough to be trusted. The 10-year Treasury yield climbed to 5.14% on September 24 as markets priced in strong business data and tighter Federal Reserve policy — proof that the bond market still holds the leash Congress has dropped. Every dollar the federal government spends is a dollar not spent by a family, a shop, or a parish. The plan: cap the deficit at two percent of GDP for a decade, then let states keep what their citizens earn, and cut every agency that cannot explain its existence in one sentence to a layman.

Command Three: Teach like the stakes are real. A boy who leaves school able to recite but unable to build, write, or command is a liability, not an asset. The plan: mastery before promotion in the first eight years; reading, writing, arithmetic, history, and one trade every student learns with his hands by sixteen; and teachers given the standing to demand effort from both students and parents. We do not need more schools. We need schools that mean it.

Command Four: Build where you can build. The chip market tells the whole story in one afternoon. On September 24, Lam Research fell 2.2%, Marvell Technology 2%, Western Digital 4.1%, and FormFactor 3.9% as yields rose — but the deeper problem is not that day's tape. It is that we debate tariffs on advanced semiconductors in a summit hall while our fabs, our rare earths, and our memory all sit across the Pacific. The plan: domestic production of anything strategic — chips, batteries, steel, medicine, grain — with subsidies paid for by ending the subsidies on things we do not need.

Command Five: Trade like you intend to win. President Trump and President Xi Jinping met in Washington and extended their tariff truce two months, to January 10, but left rare earths unresolved — no substantive pact on shipments or export licensing. A truce is not a settlement. The plan: keep the truce where it works, hold the line where it does not, and build the rare-earth and semiconductor supply chains at home so that the next summit is a choice, not a necessity.

Command Six: Fund the sword. The Department of Defense's FY2026 budget request stands at $961.6 billion — a 13.4 percent increase over FY2025 — and its own words are the plan: "rebuild America's military, reestablish deterrence, and restore the warrior ethos." That is the right doctrine. The order now is to spend it on warfighting platforms and people, not on the machinery of its own administration, and to keep spending until the adversary stops testing.

Command Seven: Let the churches ring again. Faith is not decoration on a civilization; it is the mortar between the bricks. Sunday mass, Friday night football, and the local bank are the three institutions that actually hold a town together. The plan: tax code that rewards the family and the church, zoning that lets a congregation stand where a parking lot sits, and public life that does not mock what its citizens believe when the cameras go off.

None of this is soft, and none of it is new. Nobunaga unified Japan with fewer commands than these; Ieyasu finished the job by refusing to die on principle. The West has the same seven problems in the same order it always has: too many children unafforded, too much state in the middle, schools that do not mean it, factories abroad, treaties without teeth, a sword underfunded, and a God who is only invited for the wedding.

The plan is not hard to see. It is hard to execute, which is why it has been waiting.
