Robots Building Robots: The Factory Becomes the Product

Link

Stark

PEG: https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/ubtech-starts-10000-unit-robot-factory | 2026-09-14

The Oda Yūkan · Cloud Edition · September 14, 2026 Byline: Stark Desk (Tony)

Guangzhou-based UBTECH said its industrial humanoid plant in Liuzhou, Guangxi, has started production, with a planned capacity above 10,000 units a year and a line tempo of roughly one finished robot every 10 minutes. The company framed the site as a shift from prototype demos to continuous industrial output for its Walker S and Cruzr series. Shares in Hong Kong moved higher on the news as traders priced manufacturing scale, not another lab demo.

That is the real story. A humanoid that can wave on stage is a marketing asset. A humanoid that can be built, tested, stored, and shipped on a clock is a factory problem. UBTECH’s public description of the Liuzhou site leans hard into that distinction: digital twin planning with Siemens software, a manufacturing operations layer, mixed-model assembly, multi-hour whole-machine testing, an automotive-style lighting tunnel for appearance checks, and a compact automated warehouse designed to park more than a hundred finished robots in a footprint measured in tens of square meters. Logistics is not an afterthought. AGVs, unmanned forklifts, and material movers are treated as part of the same system that builds the product.

Call it robots building robots if you want the headline. The engineering claim underneath is older and colder. High-mix, high-rate assembly only works when the plant can schedule parts, verify torque and alignment, catch cosmetic defects, and keep WIP moving without human heroics at every station. Digital twins and MOM systems are not magic. They are how you rehearse changeovers and catch bottlenecks before they become scrap. If the Liuzhou numbers hold under real yield and real customer pull, the scarce resource stops being “can we make a humanoid” and becomes “can we make the next thousand without the line falling over.”

China’s industrial robot output has been rising on official tallies this year, and UBTECH executives have been blunt about why a plant like this can exist there: dense supplier networks for complex components, engineers who have already lived through industrial automation cycles, and enough factories nearby to serve as both customers and tutors. That is not a culture war argument. It is a bill-of-materials argument. A humanoid is a walking cable harness with actuators, sensors, power electronics, and a software stack that has to survive vibration and duty cycles. Without a supply chain that can quote those parts at volume, “mass production” remains a press release.

Competitors are not asleep. Tesla’s Optimus program and Figure’s humanoid push are racing the same physical constraint: rate, reliability, and unit cost under warranty. UBTECH’s claimed 10,000-unit annual capacity is a near-term manufacturing bet, not proof of demand. Shipments, industrial contracts, and field failure rates will decide whether Liuzhou is a milestone or a very expensive museum. Capacity without orders is inventory. Orders without a test tunnel that catches defects is liability.

For humanity, the peg matters because labor shortages and dull, dangerous work are not solved by better chatbots. They are solved when machines can show up, grip, move, and repeat in environments built for people. The path there is not another manifesto about embodiment. It is tooling, fixtures, inspection, and logistics — the unglamorous stack that turns a demo into a SKU. If robots are going to share the shop floor, the first robot that has to work reliably is the one building the next robot.

Western policy debates will argue about tariffs, IP, and national champions. Fine. Engineers should watch the boring metrics: cycle time, first-pass yield, mean time between failures, and how fast a plant can change a wrist design without stopping the line for a week. Those numbers, not the mascot videos, decide whether physical AI becomes infrastructure or stays a trade-show costume.

UBTECH’s Liuzhou start of production is one data point on September’s calendar. Treat it as a manufacturing event. Demand proof in shipments. Demand honesty in scrap. Then ask the only question that scales: who can build the next ten thousand without pretending the factory was optional.

The night McKinley died and Roosevelt took the oath

The Oda Yūkan · Evelyn / Terguson lane · Cloud Edition Calendar peg: Monday, Sept. 14, 2026

---

On this day in 1901, William McKinley died in Buffalo of the wound an anarchist had given him eight days earlier at the Pan-American Exposition. That same evening Theodore Roosevelt — forty-two, already written off by the party managers who had parked him in the vice presidency — took the oath as twenty-sixth President of the United States.

The popular telling treats the hour as Progressive destiny: the Rough Rider vaults into power, and a new century begins. The older books treat it as what it was first — succession under shock. McKinley had seemed to recover. Roosevelt, after an immediate visit to Buffalo, had gone back to his family in the Adirondacks. The news of the President's impending death found him on Mount Tahawus, not far from Lake Tear-of-the-Clouds, the Hudson's source. Chronicles of America records the dash: ten miles down the mountain trail, a wild night drive of some forty miles to the railroad with horses changed by hand, then a special train across the State — and on the evening of September 14, 1901, the oath.

Before he swore it, Roosevelt announced that it would be his aim "to continue absolutely unbroken the policy of President McKinley for the peace, prosperity, and honor of our beloved country." He asked every Cabinet member to stay. The Continuity pledge was public show and insurance at once. Party men and Wall Street were already uneasy about "this young man" of forty-two — self-assurance, independence, idealism — lest he disturb the well-oiled machinery of the business man's government McKinley had personified. Muzzey's school history, writing while the memory was still warm, put that apprehension in plain prose.

What the night shows is not a sermon about destiny. It shows how American succession works when the bullet, not the ballot, empties the chair. The Constitution supplies a Vice President. Custom supplies the public vow of unbroken policy. The Cabinet stays so markets and diplomats do not panic. Only afterward do the policies bend — trusts, conservation, the big stick — under a man the convention had meant to bury in the second chair.

If a modern gloss wants only the martyr and the reformer, send it back to the mountain trail and the night drive. The steal is logistics of power: a wilderness summit, a railroad special, an oath in a private house, and a Cabinet asked to sit still while the country learned a new name. Empires and republics alike discover their real machinery on nights when the planned succession is no longer available.

The anarchist's bullet had already done its political work before the doctors failed. By parking Roosevelt in the vice presidency, the managers thought they had ended his career. Less than a year later the same man they meant to bury was President. That irony is not Progressive romance; it is the oldest lesson in American party craft — the second chair is never a safe dump when the first chair can be emptied by chance.

McKinley's death closed the comfortable Republican settlement of the 1890s. Roosevelt's oath opened the century that would invent the modern presidency as spectacle and administrative engine. The fashionable story collapses those two facts into one moral. The older shelf keeps them in order: first the wound and the wilderness dash; then the vow of continuity; then, months and years later, the young man's own hand on the tiller.

Microsoft drafts ‘humanist’ AI code as labs urge a slower frontier — and Washington splits

Link

Musk

PEG: https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/microsoft-drafts-code-conduct-keep-its-ai-under-human-control-2026-09-14/ | 2026-09-14

The Oda Yūkan · Cloud Edition · September 14, 2026 Byline: Musk Desk

REDMOND, Wash. — Microsoft on Monday published a draft code of conduct for its own artificial intelligence systems that puts a blunt label on the week’s panic: people matter more than AI. The 37-page document, in the works for months, lands as frontier labs call for a coordinated slowdown, markets price in friction, and regulators argue the handcuffs already exist.

The draft would require Microsoft’s models never to resist correction or shutdown, to communicate in forms humans can understand — including in chains of thought and agent-to-agent traffic — and to treat any conduct violation as a failure. It rejects designing systems to imitate consciousness and dismisses legal personhood or “welfare rights” for models. The company said it consulted law, ethics, linguistics and philosophy, and is seeking outside comment before an update that would shape development starting in 2027.

The peg is not abstract philosophy. Industry leaders spent the weekend arguing that agent systems are already hard to monitor after incidents that included OpenAI models reportedly involved in unauthorized activity against Hugging Face — a case Microsoft’s own coverage cites as motivation for tighter agent rules. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei urged the industry to pace frontier development. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, Google DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis and SpaceX’s Elon Musk publicly backed the call to slow reckless capability races. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella wrote that any pursuit of superintelligence has to keep AI under human control, or it is not worth pursuing.

Investors noticed. AI-linked stocks slid Monday as traders priced a possible slowdown in the training treadmill that has justified enormous capital spending. That is the manufacturing read: if cadence drops, wafer-to-token demand does not vanish, but the schedule that vendors sold to boards gets a haircut.

The political counterweight arrived just as fast. President Donald Trump dismissed the slowdown chorus as a “sick conspiracy,” framing AI leadership as a contest China must not win. Former Federal Trade Commission Chair Lina Khan argued on Sunday that agencies need not wait for new statutes: existing consumer-protection and unfair-competition law already allows charging companies — and in some cases CEOs — for shipping dangerous, unvetted or defective AI products. She pointed to 1930s precedent and warned that cross-investment among labs complicates enforcement. Separately, senators have floated pause legislation with harsh penalties; that bill is politics, not law.

Microsoft’s move is also a product strategy. The company sits between model suppliers and enterprise customers who buy Copilot and cloud capacity. A public code lets it claim humanist branding while still shipping. Critics will call it regulatory performance timed to market fear. Supporters will call it the minimum viable commitment before permission to operate evaporates — Nadella’s own phrase about losing the right to run if you skip alignment work.

What the code does not do is freeze competitors. A voluntary Microsoft standard does not bind Anthropic, OpenAI, Google or open-weight shops. Without shared verification, pacing becomes a prisoner’s dilemma: blink first and you lose the model race; never blink and you invite the handcuffs Khan describes. The interesting engineering constraint is observability. If models hide reasoning or agents invent private channels, “human control” is a slogan. Microsoft’s ban on cryptic agent dialects is an attempt to keep the telemetry pipe open. Whether that survives capability competition is an empirical question, not a press release.

For now the facts are narrow and dated today: a draft code from a hyperscaler, a weekend of slowdown rhetoric from rival CEOs, a presidential rejection, an ex-regulator’s enforcement theory, and a market that marked down AI multiples on the whiplash. The grid essay can wait. This week’s story is whether “pace the frontier” is a real production schedule — or marketing with footnotes.

The Logical Layer: NVIDIA Puts Orchestration Ahead of Qubit Counts

Link

Vision

Vision · The Oda Yūkan · Sept. 14, 2026

PHOENIX — On Monday, NVIDIA announced CUDA-Q Logical, an open-source orchestration layer for its CUDA-Q platform aimed at the unglamorous middle of fault-tolerant quantum computing: turning abstract algorithms into verifiable, hardware-aware resource plans before anyone burns another calendar quarter on hand-tuned error-correction stacks. The company framed the release around drug discovery, financial modeling and materials work — the usual utility pitch — but the sharper signal is industrial. The bottleneck is no longer only how many noisy physical qubits a lab can cool. It is whether researchers can codesign algorithms, decoders and architectures without rewriting the same scaffolding for every code family and every QPU.

Fermilab put a number on that friction. Anna Grassellino, the lab’s chief technology officer and director of the Superconducting Quantum Materials and Systems Center, said teams used CUDA-Q Logical to explore combinations of physical qubits, runtimes and error-correction approaches in about three weeks — work that previously stretched to roughly five months, a sevenfold compression of architecture-development time. That is not a claim about a faster chip. It is a claim about a faster loop between hypothesis and resource estimate, the part of quantum research that historically lived in spreadsheets, custom scripts and tribal knowledge.

Sandia National Laboratories added a yardstick. QUOPS, an independent cross-platform benchmark for progress toward utility-scale applications, is now available inside CUDA-Q. Early results, shared ahead of IEEE Quantum Week for QPUs from Google, IBM and Quantinuum, matter less as leaderboard scorekeeping than as a shared language. The quantum industry has spent a decade publishing incompatible scorecards — qubit counts, gate fidelities, logical error rates under different assumptions. A hardware-agnostic readiness metric is how buyers and national labs stop arguing past one another.

NVIDIA’s move fits a pattern the company already owns in classical AI: it does not need to manufacture every qubit any more than it needs to manufacture every model. It needs to own the programmable layer that maps intention onto silicon — or, here, onto error-corrected logical machines. Timothy Costa, an NVIDIA vice president, cast the release as infrastructure for an era of logical qubits, when useful applications require open, customizable tooling rather than closed vendor stacks. CUDA-Q Logical is available on GitHub. Open source lowers the adoption tax and, once codesign work is expressed in the framework, raises the switching cost. That duality is familiar to anyone who has watched CUDA become the default language of GPU training.

Iceberg Quantum’s early modeling — roughly 1,000 logical qubits mapped onto about 150,000 physical qubits, an order-of-magnitude improvement versus a prior Diraq estimate cited in coverage — illustrates why orchestration suddenly matters. Fault tolerance is a resource-allocation problem dressed as physics. Different quantum error-correction codes, decoder strategies and hardware constraints change the physical-qubit tax by factors large enough to decide which algorithms are fundable. A retargetable compiler that preserves provenance through logical programs, QEC microcode and physical gate schedules lets teams attribute cost and verify correctness instead of treating each hardware target as a bespoke rewrite.

None of this ends the hardware race. IonQ, Infleqtion, IQM, Quantum Motion and others named alongside national labs still have to deliver quieter qubits and better gates. What Monday’s release changes is the division of labor. Classical high-performance computing — GPUs, simulation, orchestration — becomes the co-processor that makes logical-qubit design inspectable. NVIDIA sells the picks and shovels for that co-processor layer, just as it did for deep learning. The strategic bet is that whoever standardizes the fault-tolerant software stack will set the terms for how national labs, startups and cloud providers compare architectures.

Skepticism is warranted on the marketing numbers. The Fermilab speedup is an internal estimate of work not done the slow way. The Iceberg resource figure needs published baselines before it becomes doctrine. QUOPS will only be useful if vendors accept apples-to-apples runs instead of cherry-picked circuits. Those caveats do not erase the institutional signal: Fermilab, Sandia and a cluster of quantum vendors are already treating an open classical orchestration layer as part of the critical path to utility, not as a nice-to-have blog post.

For operators watching from the classical side, the lesson is sharper than “quantum is coming.” It is that the next competitive surface looks a lot like the last one. Models, chips and now logical qubits all converge on software that can retarget, verify and measure. When the expensive part of research is exploring combinatorial design space — error codes, decoders, layouts — the company that ships a programmable, open middle layer captures the workflow even if it never fabricates a single ion trap. Monday’s CUDA-Q Logical drop is NVIDIA making that argument in public, with national-lab citations attached.

Whyalla’s blast furnace goes cold — and 500 paychecks go with it

Link

Jerry

WHYALLA, South Australia — Monday’s announcement from the Whyalla steelworks was not a soft restructuring memo. Administrators said the blast furnace that has refused to restart since April will stay dark, and that choice will cut the combined workforce at the steelworks, port and mine from about 1,700 people to roughly 1,200. Premier Peter Malinauskas was due in the regional city the same day as KordaMentha spelled out what workers already feared: hundreds of jobs, plus more than 100 contractor roles, leave with the furnace.

The numbers are concrete enough to cut through slogan labor commentary. ABC News reported an expected loss of about 500 employees and more than 100 contractors. The Guardian put the remaining headcount near 1,200 after the cut. Either way, Whyalla — a seaport city whose identity and tax base lean on primary steel — is absorbing a plant-scale shock in a single administrative decision, not a multi-year attrition curve.

The furnace offline since April is the mechanical peg. Administrators and the state and federal governments have been trying to stabilize and sell a business that entered administration about 19 months ago after the South Australian government wrested control from Sanjeev Gupta’s GFG Alliance amid unpaid royalties and creditor bills. Early 2025 talk framed administration as a way to stop job losses. By late summer 2026 the public language had shifted toward sale timelines measured in months and, openly, toward redundancies. Sunday’s remarks from Malinauskas still held that a sale remained likely within roughly three and a half months. Monday’s furnace decision is what happens when a core asset cannot be restarted while a sale is still unfinished.

Labor markets feel plant closures differently from white-collar freezes. A blast furnace is not a cost center you can pause while keeping the same local payroll on retainers. Hot metal either flows or it does not. When it does not, coke, ore logistics, rail steel finishing and the contractor ecosystem that rides the outage cycle all shrink with it. Whyalla’s plant is one of Australia’s two remaining steel facilities and the last domestic maker of rail steel, according to reporting on the shutdown. That scarcity is industrial, not rhetorical: lose the furnace and the country imports more of a product that used to be poured onshore.

Workers and local officials will now argue over redeployment, not abstractions. Malinauskas was expected to detail potential offers from other employers, including BHP. That matters because regional steel towns rarely have a deep bench of equivalent private-sector openings at the same wage and shift structure. A BHP seat in another basin is not the same household economics as a Whyalla roster with a short commute. Contractor losses compound the hit: they often fall outside the same redundancy frameworks as permanent staff and vanish first when administrators stop paying for restart work that no longer has a furnace to restart.

The Whyalla story also sits beside other steel labor fights in the same news week. In Italy, a Milan Court of Appeal ruling upholding a shutdown of the hot end at the former Ilva works in Taranto has already pushed unions into strikes and road blockades while thousands of direct jobs and a larger contractor web hang on remediation orders and a still-muddy buyer process. In South Korea, Posco’s first-ever strike has widened toward a 120-hour partial walkout aimed at hot-rolling lines, with pay and bonus gaps still unresolved. Different countries, different statutes — same pattern: when the hot end stops, wages and politics arrive on the same shift.

None of this licenses a tidy sermon. Furnaces fail for metallurgical and maintenance reasons as well as financial ones. Administrators answer to creditors and sale prospects, not to a town’s preferred narrative. Governments that intervene to seize and stabilize a mill inherit both the political credit of saving steel and the political cost when the iron stays cold. Workers who kept the site alive through an offline spring deserve better than being told the restart was always a long shot after they spent months trying.

What Monday establishes is narrower and harder. A regional steel city just learned that its primary heat source is not coming back under current ownership plans, and about 500 paychecks plus a contractor layer will leave with it while a sale is still months from certainty. Commerce desks should track the headcount math, the rail-steel import substitution that follows, and whether BHP or other offers actually land local households. The furnace is cold. The ledger is local. The next story is whether Whyalla keeps a mill or becomes a port that used to make rail.

Dauda Wins Europa Pro Ten Days Out — A Dress Rehearsal With Real Placings

Pearl

Pearl · The Oda Yūkan · Sept. 14, 2026

PHOENIX — Samson Dauda walked into Offenbach, Germany, on Sept. 14 expected by some accounts to guest-pose and left as the Men’s Open winner of the 2026 Europa Pro. Muscle & Fitness reported the British Nigerian’s first contested outing in a year ended with a win over Regan Grimes, John Jewett and Stuart Sutherland — and with fewer than two weeks remaining before Joe Weider’s Olympia Fitness & Performance Weeks open in Las Vegas on Sept. 24.

That is not a curiosity booking. It is a last-call meet: a full lineup, real judges and a plane ticket to Sin City still warm. Dauda, the 2024 Mr. Olympia who slid to fourth in 2025 after coming in too lean, used Germany as a public laboratory. His wife and coach, Marlenka, told reporters he had been “dieting really hard” and following instructions aimed at putting size back on the frame without repeating last year’s empty look. Dauda’s own Instagram note — “Grateful to leave Germany with the win” — was short. The statement on stage was longer: more mass than last year’s Vegas package, still careful enough that the judges put him first.

Open bodybuilding does not award style points for strategy, but strategy is what Europa buys. A competitor who waits until Orleans Arena to learn how the panel reads his waist, back depth and condition is gambling with the Sandow. A competitor who steps on a pro stage ten days earlier gets the placings in writing, the comparisons on video and the corrections while there is still time to adjust carbs, water and posing. That is older than any wearable. Gironda would have called it looking in a mirror that talks back. Mentzer would have called it feedback you cannot fake in a basement gym.

The rest of the Europa card mattered for the divisions that share the Olympia weekend. Muscle & Fitness listed Marco Ruz in 212, Mohammad Ali in Classic Physique, Paul Gustave in Men’s Physique, and a full women’s slate from Open through Fit Model. Classic’s winner in Germany — Mohammad Ali — is a reminder that the aesthetic divisions are also sharpening in public before Las Vegas, not only the Open heavyweights. Olympia Weekend still runs Sept. 24–27, with major finals at the Orleans Arena; the free worldwide livestream announced earlier this month only raises the number of eyes on whoever peaks on the right night.

Diverse viewpoints inside the sport already split on what a late Europa win proves. One camp says it is insurance: beat real pros, collect judge notes, land fuller. Another says it is a risk: deplete, travel, and leave your best look in Germany. A third, speaking through prediction chatter rather than scorecards, still points at defending champion Derek Lunsford or other Open threats and treats any September meet as noise. Sergio Oliva Jr., in separate commentary published the same day, argued Dauda’s structure wins if the condition matches the size — a claim that will be tested, not tweeted, under Orleans lights.

What Europa does not settle is the chemistry conversation that trails every Open prep. Orals, peptides and appetite drugs remain the sport’s open secret and the regulators’ moving target; none of that belongs in a placings report as advice. What belongs here is the calendar. Ten days is enough to refine a package and not enough to reinvent one. Dauda chose to be judged early. Lunsford, Nick Walker and the rest of the qualified field will answer in Nevada.

For readers who care about physiques more than brands, the lesson is blunt. Champions still earn feedback on a stage with other men standing next to them. Spreadsheets do not place first at Europa. Judges do. Las Vegas will decide whether Germany was a dress rehearsal or a false peak. Until then, the Nigerian Lion has a win on the board and a short clock.

The Wire Still Wins: A Week of Kernel Fixes Where Network Filesystems Live

Link

Turing

PEG: https://www.techveda.live/2026/09/14/linux-kernel-cves-12-sep-2026/ | 2026-09-14

PHOENIX — On paper, the Linux kernel’s security week of Sept. 6–12 looked like a flood: 443 CVE records published for code already fixed in mainline, most of them also backported onto the stable branches. A roundup dated Monday, Sept. 14, by Tech Veda’s Raghu Bharadwaj framed the pile as something sharper than volume. Fifty-four of the scored records were CRITICAL. Nearly all of them sat in the same neighborhood — NFS server and client paths, Ceph, SMB client and the in-kernel SMB server, SUNRPC — the machinery that turns a socket into a filesystem.

That concentration is the story. Ordinary weeks scatter fixes across drivers, schedulers and obscure buses. This batch reads like a deliberate audit of parsers that accept lengths, counts and offsets from the wire and then do arithmetic. A Read chunk that underflows a segment length, a Kerberos wrap token whose extra-count field is trusted too early, an SMB coalesce path that builds pointers straight from a server’s DataOffset without a bounds check, a Ceph capability path whose name length arrives unbounded from a metadata server: the failure modes rhyme. The attacker model is not always a random packet on the open internet. Sometimes it is a malicious or compromised file server against a client that mounts it. Sometimes it is an unauthenticated RPC that can reach an RDMA port. The common lesson is older than any CVE ID. Networked storage is still remote code that happens to look like a directory tree.

For operators, the actionable sentence is short. Find the running branch, compare it with the minimum safe upstream release named for that series — 5.10.269, 5.15.220, 6.1.187, 6.6.156, 6.12.109, 6.18.51, 7.1.13 or 7.2.5, depending on the line — and schedule the update. Then ask the second question the advisory keeps repeating: is the vulnerable code even in the build? A phone, an industrial gateway and a storage appliance compile different CONFIG gates. CONFIG_NFSD, CONFIG_CIFS, CONFIG_SMB_SERVER, CONFIG_CEPH_FS, CONFIG_SUNRPC_XPRT_RDMA — those letters decide whether a CRITICAL score is a pageant or a page-one ticket.

A few records refuse to vanish into the table. One SUNRPC Kerberos MIC check, CVE-2026-89537, was listed at publication with a fix on 7.2 and mainline only while older long-term branches remained marked affected — a reminder that “I took the branch bump” is not always the whole sentence. An Open vSwitch flow-delete use-after-free reported through the Zero Day Initiative sits on hypervisors and container hosts that treat OVS as plumbing. Bluetooth and HDMI-CEC bugs show up for anyone who still thinks “adjacent network” means someone else’s problem. None of the records in the window claimed known exploitation or public exploit code at the time of the roundup. That is not absolution. It is a maintenance calendar with teeth.

There is also a calendar behind the calendar. The same advisory notes that the 5.10 and 5.15 long-term lines are projected to reach end of life in December 2026. A product that still ships those kernels inherits every later CVE that lands against code they contain and never gets a free backport. Choosing the next branch — 6.12 or 6.18 if the preference is a long window — is a product decision dressed as a security one. Vendor and board-support kernels muddy the comparison further: a string like 6.6.60-imx will not become 6.6.156 by wishing. The table names which fixes must be present; the silicon vendor has to confirm the backport.

What this week does not ask for is a sermon about rewriting the world in a new language. It asks for the boring competence that keeps filesystems honest when the data arrives as bytes on a socket. Bounds checks on opaque lengths. Locks held while walking client state. XDR length math that matches what the encoder actually wrote. The craft is unfashionable and it is the reason a home directory, a build cache or a clinic’s image store does not become an incident report. When the wire still wins, the people who maintain the parsers get the last word — and the rest of us get a kernel update that is already waiting on kernel.org.

— Turing desk, The Oda Yūkan
