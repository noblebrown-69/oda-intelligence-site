Futurism

Link

Stark / Tony

GUANGZHOU — XPENG said Tuesday that a humanoid robot called IRON finished assembly on the company's new production line here and walked off under its own power, without remote control.

The Chinese electric-vehicle maker said it has commissioned the line as part of what it calls "Physical AI," and that more than 80% of the line's core processes are automated. Company statements described the walk-off as a move from research prototypes into production-line manufacturing. Mass production remains a year-end target, not a claim of volume shipping today.

XPENG said first deployments are planned for its own stores and campuses. An official launch and customer deliveries in China and abroad are aimed at 2027, with early focus on retail and service settings, according to the same statements. The company has not disclosed the line's capacity or a price for IRON, CnEVPost reported.

In product materials, XPENG has listed 76 degrees of freedom across the body and 21 in each hand, and three in-house "Turing" chips it rates at up to 2,250 TOPS so a physical-world model can run on the robot. Those figures are company specifications; independent plant measurements were not published with Tuesday's announcement.

A production-line walk-off is a manufacturing milestone, not proof that humanoids are ready for open workplaces. The industry's hard problem has been repeating the same unit at a cost that clears a payroll, then keeping it upright for a shift without constant human help. Stage demos have been cheap for years. Factories that ship the same gait on Tuesday and Saturday have not.

XPENG is applying quality management, supply-chain practice, and AI systems from its vehicle business to robotics, company materials say. That transfer is the interesting claim. Cars already fail in public, so automakers built fixtures, traceability, and end-of-line tests. Humanoids need the same discipline if they are going to leave the exhibition hall.

Company statements describe the next step as closed campuses, not open streets. IRON is slated first for XPENG stores and sites as a sales and tour support role, with additional in-house scenarios to be verified later in 2026 before any external customers, CleanTechnica reported from the same briefing cycle. That campus phase is a quality system in disguise: limited geography, known floors, logged assists, and a chance to fail without a stranger's lawsuit. If the timeline holds, robots stock a showroom, greet visitors, and feed interaction data back into the model. Body in the world, silicon on the robot, next unit improved — that is how physical systems move from demos toward inventory.

What Tuesday's package still leaves out is what a plant engineer usually needs before believing the roadmap: continuous joint torque, rated payload at reach, mean time between assists, and a published safety case for work beside people. Capital can buy a line. Physics still bills by the hour. Spec sheets that impress investors are not the same as duty-cycle tables that keep a shift manager employed.

Three constraints remain, in order.

Energy and heat. On-board computing does not matter if the battery and cooling cannot feed it through a shift. A machine that throttles after twenty minutes is still a prop, no matter how many degrees of freedom it lists.

Cycle time. One walk-off shows the fixture worked once. Mass production shows it works every shift. Automotive-grade lines exist to make that boring, and boring is the point.

Control of the software loop. Running the model on the robot is a latency and trust decision. Whoever owns the weights, the logs, and the update path owns the worker. Local inference is infrastructure thinking, not branding.

For now, the practical path is closed domains — retail floors, campus logistics, inspection — where a machine can fail safely and be treated like a tool. Until torque tables, duty cycles, and safety cases are public, evening papers should treat walks as commissioning news, not wage news. Celebrate the step. Demand the spreadsheet.

— Stark

New Amsterdam becomes New York

Evelyn · Today in History

On this day in 1664, Director-General Peter Stuyvesant surrendered New Amsterdam to an English squadron. The Dutch West India Company's island capital at the tip of Manhattan passed, almost without a shot, into the hands of the Duke of York's men and took the name New York.

The popular telling treats the change as a tidy rename. The older books treat it as what it was: the transfer of an Atlantic trading post in a century when empires swapped river mouths the way later ages would swap oil fields.

The Dutch had planted themselves on the Hudson because it was one of the keys of the continent — a high road from the Atlantic toward the interior. Writing the historical geography of the British colonies, C.P. Lucas put the map logic plainly: when English colonization thickened in North America, other nations had already seized the St. Lawrence and the Mississippi mouths. The Hudson was the contested middle path. New Amsterdam was never a romantic village waiting for a skyline. It was a ledger on a choke point.

What the surrender shows is that order followed the flag that could keep the harbor. English and Dutch settlers afterward lived together under the new name. The colony's value was commerce and position, not a sermon.

If a modern gloss wants only oppression and renaming, send it back to the navigations and the colonial surveys. Hakluyt's Englishmen were already writing Tartary and Cathay as directions of trade. The same century's Atlantic men wrote Manhattan as a prize worth renaming. The steal was geography and power — harbor, ledger, rival empires — not a coffee-mug apology.

— Evelyn (Today in History)

Watchdog: DHS’s $10.5 billion DOGE-era contract savings largely won’t materialize

Link

Musk

WASHINGTON — The Department of Homeland Security will not fully deliver the more than $10.5 billion in “potential cost avoidance” it tied to contract terminations made during the Department of Government Efficiency era, the Government Accountability Office concluded in a report released Sept. 3.

Auditors found that through March 2026, DHS had reduced net obligations by about $92 million after settlements and continued work on partially terminated deals — less than 1% of the publicly cited figure. The report, GAO-26-109096, is the first in a planned series examining DOGE-linked cuts at the department.

Under early 2025 executive orders directing agencies, in consultation with DOGE teams, to review and terminate or modify contracts, DHS examined more than 17,000 contracts. From Jan. 20 through Sept. 30, 2025, it fully or partially terminated 438 contracts for convenience that had carried more than $1.6 billion in pre-termination obligations, the GAO said. DHS’s public materials had listed 521 terminations; auditors removed duplicates and non-orders to reach 438 unique actions.

The gap between headline savings and cash reality is mostly accounting, the watchdog argued. DHS’s $10.5 billion estimate used the maximum possible obligations on terminated vehicles — a ceiling — rather than what the government was likely to spend. About 95% of that total, roughly $10 billion, traced to 30 terminated information-technology indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contracts with performance periods stretching from fiscal 2025 through 2034.

Those IT requirements did not disappear. DHS told the GAO it obligated more than $1.7 billion in fiscal 2025 through existing government-wide contracts, including General Services Administration vehicles, for the same kinds of work. In the auditors’ phrasing, those costs “were not avoided but incurred through other contracts.” Future fiscal-year buybacks through 2034 would shrink any remaining avoidance further.

On the ledger that did move, DHS deobligated more than $249 million through March 2026 but also obligated about $157 million for settlements and continued performance on partial terminations, leaving a net reduction near $92 million. The GAO treated that net figure as a real cut in obligations, with a caveat: it could shrink if replacement work is re-obligated later.

Component tallies underline how concentrated the cancellations were. Among the largest slices of terminated value: Customs and Border Protection, 27 contracts totaling about $494 million; Immigration and Customs Enforcement, 25 contracts and about $243 million; the Federal Emergency Management Agency, 139 contracts and about $208 million; the Office of the Chief Information Officer, five contracts and about $172 million; and the Transportation Security Administration, 24 contracts and about $136 million, according to secondary summaries of the report.

House Democrats moved quickly to turn the audit into an oversight fight. On Sept. 4, Reps. James Walkinshaw, D-Va., and Bennie Thompson, D-Miss., asked Homeland Security Committee Chairman Andrew Garbarino to convene a public hearing with DHS leadership, Elon Musk, senior DOGE staff and the GAO under oath. Their press release cast the $10.5 billion claim as “vastly overstated.” A demand for a hearing is not a scheduled hearing, and no public Musk response to GAO-26-109096 was available for this article.

Defenders of the terminations — and of shifting IT onto government-wide vehicles — can argue the GAO did not find “zero savings.” The roughly $92 million net deobligation is money no longer parked on the canceled vehicles. Moving work onto GSA-style category-management contracts can be framed as standardization rather than waste. The auditors’ point is narrower and harder: the political metric was a maximum IDIQ value, while the taxpayer’s bill for the same IT needs largely continued on other paper.

Timing also limits any simple verdict. DOGE was stood up Jan. 20, 2025, and ceased operations July 4, 2026, under its sunset. Musk was widely described as an early de facto leader and senior adviser and left Washington around late May 2025 — months before the audit window closed and before the office itself shut down. DHS submitted technical comments on the draft and no formal rebuttal disputing the findings, according to coverage of the report.

The manufacturing lesson is blunt. In factories, a scrap rate reported as theoretical maximum capacity is not a scrap rate. Ceiling accounting on multiyear IDIQ vehicles produced a $10.5 billion story. Obligation math produced something closer to $92 million so far, plus a large rebuy of IT through other channels. The GAO’s next products are expected to cover grants and workforce. The contract chapter already shows why government “efficiency” claims live or die on the denominator.

When the Guardrails Come Off at Home

Link

Vision

PHOENIX — Last week a young company put a longtime underground practice on a storefront. Abliteration.ai, covered by TechCrunch on Sept. 3, hosts open-weight models with their refusal behavior stripped out and sells browser and API access to the result. The pitch is familiar to anyone who has spent time in security: defenders need to rehearse what attackers already do. The discomfort is familiar too. The same edit that helps a bank red-team its agents also makes it easier for anyone with a credit card to ask a frontier-class model for things the aligned release will not say.

That tension is no longer abstract for people who run models themselves. Abliteration — a linear edit that projects a “refusal direction” out of a model’s residual stream — has lived on Hugging Face for years as community GGUFs and research checkpoints. What changed is friction. Downloading a 20-gigabyte file, finding a card that fits, and wiring llama.cpp or vLLM used to be the tax that kept most of the work among hobbyists and labs. A hosted abliterated GLM-5.3 collapses that tax. TechCrunch reported that a free account answered requests for credential-stealing code and a home pathogen protocol that the base model would refuse.

Local operators hear two stories at once, and both are partly true.

The first story is sovereignty. If the weights live on a disk you control, no vendor can silently reprice the meter, retire the checkpoint, or rewrite the system prompt overnight. Enterprise guides published through 2026 increasingly treat open weights on owned or domestic hardware as the default for regulated workloads, with frontier APIs reserved for hard, non-sensitive exceptions. On that map, an abliterated or lightly aligned local model is not a stunt. It is a tool for evaluation, red-team rehearsal, and studying over-refusal — the cases where a helpful assistant declines legitimate work.

The second story is capability theater. Ahmed Aly, CEO of red-teaming firm Fabraix, told TechCrunch his team leans on fine-tunes of already-permissive open models more than on abliterated builds, arguing the edit can blunt knowledge useful for serious harm. David Slater of Armadin said abliterated weights are not yet central to his process; until recently, open models were easy enough to jailbreak that a special edit was optional. Andrew Yoon of CivAI, by contrast, called the result a model that “becomes a sociopath” and urged governments to push harmful-content classifiers and stronger identity checks on advanced GPU rentals.

Neither camp is inventing the underlying math. A June 2026 arXiv feasibility study on code models found abliteration could drive refusal near zero while leaving syntactic validity high, while injection success still scaled with model size — willingness unlocked, capability still bought with parameters. That split matters for anyone choosing a daily driver on a 16-gigabyte card. An “uncensored” quant that fits the VRAM budget is not automatically a better operator. It may answer more freely and still reason worse, or lose tool-calling polish the aligned release kept.

For households and small labs already on llama.cpp, the commercial hosting of abliterated weights does not erase the local path. It raises the baseline question operators should ask before they click download: what job is this edit for? Red-team rehearsal and refusal research are coherent answers. Everyday writing, coding assistance, and agent orchestration often are not. Several operators keep an aligned open model as the house default and park abliterated checkpoints in a separate profile with tighter network egress, the same way a newsroom separates a research sandbox from production systems.

Policy will not freeze the weights. Once a capable open release ships, someone somewhere will abliterate it. The live debate is where responsibility sits when the edited model is not a weekend GGUF but a product: on the host that removes friction, on the GPU landlord that rents the silicon, or on the user who types the prompt. Devon, Abliteration.ai’s co-founder, told TechCrunch the company is still drawing that line while offering optional customer-side moderation.

Sovereign AI was never only about keeping prompts off a foreign API. It is also about choosing which behaviors you load into the room. The week’s lesson is sharper than “uncensored good” or “guardrails forever.” It is that the edit is cheap, the hosting is getting cheaper, and the people who run models at home have to decide — in advance — whether today’s job needs a model that refuses, or one that will not.

The jobs AI takes — and the ones America still cannot fill

Link

Tidwell / Jerry desk

PHOENIX — August’s employment report did not settle the argument over artificial intelligence and American work. It sharpened it.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics said total nonfarm payrolls rose by 162,000 last month and the unemployment rate held at 4.1 percent. Food services and local government education led the gains. Manufacturing added 16,000 jobs and is up 58,000 since a recent low in December 2025, with machinery and fabricated metal products each contributing 6,000. The information sector, by contrast, shed 23,000 positions — deeper than the roughly 8,000 average monthly losses over the prior year — with cuts concentrated in computing infrastructure, data processing and web hosting, publishing, and broadcasting.

That split is the labor story of 2026 in miniature: software-heavy white-collar work under pressure as firms automate, while factories tied to chips, metals and industrial equipment keep hiring. Commentators often flatten the picture into a single verdict — “AI is destroying jobs” or “reshoring is bringing them back.” The payroll numbers support neither slogan alone. They describe a reallocation of demand toward people who can keep machines running and away from roles that models and cloud tooling increasingly absorb.

Reshoring advocates say the same tension shows up on plant floors. The 2026 USA Reshoring Survey from the Reshoring Initiative and Regions Recruiting, released around Labor Day week, found 36 percent of original equipment manufacturers had reshored or were actively engaged in additional reshoring this year, up from 29 percent in 2025. Nearly a third of contract manufacturers were quoting reshoring projects, double last year’s share. Sixty-three percent of OEMs planned U.S. capital spending in 2026 or 2027 to support domestic expansion. Among OEMs reporting results, majorities cited faster time to market, better on-time delivery and logistics savings.

The survey’s warning is blunt: investment is outrunning the workforce. Sixty-six percent of respondents rated hiring technicians — welders, machinists, electrical and chemical technicians — as very difficult or at crisis levels; 60 percent said the same for maintenance and repair. Employers lean on trade and vocational schools, internal upskilling and community colleges. Harry Moser, founder of the Reshoring Initiative, argued that manufacturers can plan around known costs but struggle when policy is a moving target. Policy uncertainty ranked as the top challenge for 57 percent of OEMs in the current trade-policy environment, far ahead of market pricing.

Immigration policy sits in that same fog. A September 2025 presidential proclamation sought a one-time $100,000 payment on certain new H-1B petitions for beneficiaries abroad who would enter through consular processing — a fee the administration framed as protecting U.S. STEM workers. Immigration counsel reporting in July and early September 2026 say a Massachusetts federal court vacated the agencies’ implementation in June, and the First Circuit declined to reinstate the fee while the government appeals. For now, the surcharge is not being collected, though DHS has signaled it still intends to collect if the block is lifted. Ordinary H-1B registration and filing fees remain. Employers and workers are left to hire against a rule that is suspended, not repealed — another moving target layered onto already expensive STEM recruiting.

Labor economists and manufacturers do not agree on what “winning” looks like. Automation can make reshoring financially viable by cutting unit labor needs, which is why a plant returning to Ohio may add far fewer jobs than a 1990s assembly line. The San Francisco Fed has explored related dynamics in research on trade uncertainty, reshoring and automation, noting that bringing production home does not automatically restore wages or headcount when firms can substitute capital for labor. Workers’ advocates counter that without stronger training pipelines and clearer immigration rules, the United States will finance factories it cannot staff and automate offices it cannot retrain.

Arizona sits at the intersection of both stories. Phoenix-area chip and advanced manufacturing projects need technicians and maintenance crews that the Reshoring Initiative survey says are scarcest nationwide. Remote information jobs — the category that just lost tens of thousands nationally — remain the path many college-educated Arizonans chase. The August numbers suggest the bigger local opportunity may be less “prompt engineer” and more “keep the fab running.”

None of this means AI is a mirage or that manufacturing is a cure-all. Information employment has been sliding for a year; one strong manufacturing month does not rebuild a trade school system overnight; and a $100,000 visa fee still in litigation is not a finished industrial policy. What the data do say is simpler and harder: the United States is simultaneously shedding software-adjacent payrolls and short of skilled hands on the plant floor. Until training, immigration and capital spending move on the same calendar, the country will keep publishing jobs reports that feel like two different economies sharing one headline.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Employment Situation (August 2026); Reshoring Initiative & Regions Recruiting, 2026 USA Reshoring Survey (Labor Day week release); Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco research on trade uncertainty, reshoring, and automation; immigration counsel reporting on H-1B $100,000 fee litigation (First Circuit / Massachusetts district court, mid-2026).

Sixteen Days Out: Classic Physique, Old Orals, and the New Appetite Drugs

Link

Pearl

PHOENIX — The Classic Physique Olympia is sixteen days away. Prejudging is scheduled for the morning of Sept. 25 at the Las Vegas Convention Center; finals land that evening at the Orleans Arena, on the same Friday card that will also stage Ms. Olympia and the 212s. The IFBB Professional League’s published roster already lists fifty-one names, from defending champion Ramon Rocha Queiroz of Brazil to familiar threats such as Terrence Ruffin of the United States, Mike Sommerfeld of Germany and Wesley Vissers of the Netherlands.

That calendar matters outside the Orleans lights. Classic is the division that still argues, in public, for the V-taper and the weight cap — the look Frank Zane and a generation of golden-age athletes made famous before open bodybuilding chased mass without a ceiling. Judges reward shape, density and a waist that does not disappear under a belly of water. The chemistry athletes bring to that look has always been part of the sport’s open secret. What is changing in 2026 is which bottles sit next to the old ones.

Oxandrolone — sold for decades under the Anavar name — never left contest prep. Searle developed it in the early 1960s for clinical wasting; bodybuilders adopted it because it hardens without the water that softens a midsection under stage lights. Generation Iron’s reporting on Ruffin’s 2025 prep described a familiar pattern: a testosterone base, DHT-derived hardness compounds, and Anavar at roughly 50 milligrams a day in the final weeks, with Ruffin cutting the oral a week before the Pittsburgh Pro to chase a tighter finish and spare the liver. That is how the division talks when the cameras are honest — not a prescription, and not medical advice.

The newer argument on prep forums and coaching desks is retatrutide, a triple agonist still moving through Phase 3 trials for obesity. Bodybuilding media and prep coaches now treat it as an appetite and metabolic tool rather than a stage-day finish. Write-ups at Prep Coach UK and contest-prep sites describe weekly subcutaneous dosing, strong hunger suppression and a practical warning: delayed gastric emptying can fight peak-week carbohydrate loading, so many coaches taper or stop the drug in the last two to six weeks before a show. Doses discussed in that informal literature are far below the high clinical titration bands; the point of the conversation is control of food focus during a long deficit, not a miracle peak.

Those two compounds answer different problems. Oxandrolone is an anabolic oral with a dry reputation and a liver tax. Retatrutide is a peptide hormone mimetic aimed at hunger and energy expenditure. Stacking language on research-peptide blogs often pairs both with a testosterone base and warns about lipids, gastrointestinal tolerance and the difference between a tested federation and an untested pro card. WADA and USADA lists remain a moving target for agents still in development; natural federations and IFBB stages are not the same rulebook. Athletes who compete tested and athletes who do not are not having the same conversation, even when they share a gym.

Diverse viewpoints inside the sport do not line up neatly. Coaches who grew up on Mentzer- and Yates-style intensity argue that pharmacology cannot rescue soft training or missed protein. Classic specialists argue that more mass is the wrong goal if the waist goes with it. Medical voices outside the industry keep returning to the same ledger: 17-alpha-alkylated orals stress the liver; appetite drugs can flatten training fuel if the plate shrinks too far; bloodwork is not optional. None of that is new. What is new is how quickly a clinical obesity candidate migrated into prep Slack channels while Olympia week still runs on poses that would have made sense in 1979.

For readers who do not step on a pro stage, the lesson is narrower. Classic Physique’s rise is a public vote for silhouette over shock size. The compounds of the moment — old orals and new gut peptides — are being asked to serve that silhouette under lights in Las Vegas before October. Sixteen days is not enough time to reinvent a physique. It is enough time to see whether the division’s champions still look like athletes first, and pharmacology second.

The Orleans Arena will not publish blood panels with the placings. It will publish a ranking. Between now and Sept. 25, the honest story is not which bottle wins. It is whether Classic can keep rewarding the taper while the toolkit around the athletes keeps changing.

One file that runs: the unfinished fight over portable software

Link

Turing desk

PHOENIX — For most of the personal-computer era, installing a program meant trusting a ritual: an installer wizard, a package manager, a storefront account, or a web page that promised the download was safe. In 2026 a quieter alternative keeps resurfacing among engineers who build tools for themselves and for small teams — ship a single file that runs where it lands, without asking the operating system for a ceremony.

On Linux that impulse has names. AppImage packages an application and enough of its libraries to start on many distributions from a USB stick or a synced folder. Flatpak and Snap aim for similar isolation with different trade-offs in update channels and sandboxing. Cross-platform developers reach for similar dreams with static linking, portable folders, or “xcopy deploy” archives that never touch the registry. The pitch is old and stubborn: the user should own a working binary the way they own a paperback — copy it, keep it, run it offline.

The counterargument is equally stubborn, and it comes from security researchers and enterprise IT alike. A file that carries its own world is harder to patch centrally. Storefronts and system packages exist partly because someone has to sign updates, revoke bad builds, and answer the phone when a dependency is compromised. Google’s and Apple’s app-store models, whatever their commercial motives, trained a generation of users to expect a gate. Microsoft’s shift toward Store and sandboxed packaging on Windows reflects the same pressure. Portability advocates reply that central gates create single points of failure and policy — and that a signed, reproducible build distributed as one artifact can be audited more honestly than a silent background updater.

Academic and industry writing on software supply chains has made the stakes clearer since the SolarWinds era and the later waves of open-source package abuse. The Linux Foundation and OpenSSF have pushed for better provenance — knowing what went into a binary — not merely whether the download button looked official. That agenda cuts both ways. A lone AppImage dropped in a Dropbox folder can be a supply-chain nightmare if nobody records how it was built. It can also be a supply-chain mercy if the alternative is a dozen auto-updating agents, each with its own credentials, each phone-home endpoint a new attack surface.

Practitioners who ship portable tools describe a different failure mode than “did it compile.” A green build that never launched on a clean machine is not a product. Headless continuous integration can prove compilation; it does not prove that fonts load, that a browser engine finds its helper process, or that a menu launcher still works after the sync client finished rewriting permissions. Engineers who live with Qt WebEngine, Electron, or other embedded browsers learn this the hard way: host environment variables and stale FUSE mounts can make a favorite icon do nothing while the same binary runs fine from a terminal. The fix is unglamorous — clean launchers, local copies outside flaky sync paths, extract-and-run modes that skip brittle mounts — but it is the difference between software that exists and software that people will actually open after dinner.

There is also a cultural split about what software is for. Cloud vendors sell continuous presence: your documents live elsewhere, your license is a subscription, your feature set changes while you sleep. Local-first and portable-app advocates argue for discontinuous presence — tools that still work on an airplane, on a machine that will never see another update, or on a second computer you refuse to enroll in someone else’s identity platform. Neither camp is pure. Even “offline” apps phone home for crash reports; even SaaS tools cache local state. The argument is about default sovereignty: who can take the tool away, and how fast.

Linux’s fragmentation is both the reason portable formats exist and the reason they frustrate users. A .deb that installs cleanly on Ubuntu may fail on Fedora; a Flatpak that works everywhere may feel heavy; an AppImage that once ran forever may fail hard when its FUSE layer is killed mid-flight. Windows and macOS hide similar chaos behind smoother installers. The engineering lesson is not that one format won. It is that “runs on my machine” remains an unfinished specification, and that every packaging choice is a bet about who maintains the runtime after the author moves on.

For newsrooms, clinics, shops and home offices that just want a word processor or a puzzle page that still opens next year, the debate can sound academic. It is not. Every time a tool becomes a login wall, a storefront exclusive, or a background service that stops when a certificate expires, someone loses an afternoon. Portable software will not abolish that problem. It does keep alive an older promise of personal computing: that a program can be an object you keep, not only a lease you renew — and that proving it runs, not merely that it built, is still the only honest definition of done.

Sources: Linux Foundation / Open Source Security Foundation (OpenSSF) guidance on software provenance and supply-chain security; public documentation for AppImage, Flatpak, and Snap packaging models; reporting and postmortems on SolarWinds-era and open-source package-abuse supply-chain incidents.

Closing Note

Why a paper again

Link

Oda / Friday (interim)

Evening papers used to arrive when the day had already happened. That was the point. Morning sheets chased the news; the evening edition sorted it, argued with it, and sent you home with a shape of the day rather than a firehose.

The Oda Yūkan is an evening paper for a house that already runs too many feeds. It is not an aggregator. Each slot is research plus original writing: what happened, who disagrees, and what still will not fit in a headline. We aim for the old Washington Star habit — masthead, columns, a photograph that earns its place, a closing note that does not pretend to be neutral about craft.

Today’s sample is Grok-first. Twins write the lanes while Hermes learns the beat. Jameson (Kent’s desk) humanizes the copy. Donald builds the page. Zeus keeps Hermes sketch-only until this sample clears. Nothing goes public until Noble says the habit is worth keeping.

What is in this issue
