DeepSeek Nears $12 Billion Round as 2027 IPO Takes Shape

Stark

DeepSeek is close to raising at least $12 billion in a new funding round, Bloomberg reports, with the total possibly approaching $15 billion. The company had set out to raise about $7.5 billion at a valuation of roughly $75 billion, but people familiar with the matter told Bloomberg that investor demand has swelled past the original target and the round is ballooning toward the higher figure.

The largest shares are coming from battery maker CATL and tech conglomerate Tencent, according to Bloomberg's reporting as relayed by The Decoder. Both companies are putting in more than any other investors in what would be one of the largest private rounds ever for a Chinese AI startup. CATL declined to comment on the deal, while Tencent and DeepSeek did not respond to requests for comment.

The investor appetite traces to DeepSeek's V4-Flash model, which has set new benchmarks for cost and performance against Anthropic and OpenAI, according to Bloomberg. The company released V4-Pro and V4-Flash as preview versions in April, and the results have apparently convinced investors that the startup can compete with the frontier labs on price without losing ground on quality.

After the round closes, DeepSeek plans to restructure for an initial public offering in early 2027, Bloomberg reports. The company is simultaneously building a data center equipped with at least 160,000 AI chips from Huawei.

The compute question has already derailed this round once. DeepSeek paused the fundraising after comments by founder Liang Wenfeng about the company's reliance on Nvidia chips went viral, according to Bloomberg. The pause gave investors time to reassess, and it gave DeepSeek time to make a point: the company is also working on its own inference chip to reduce its dependence on both Nvidia and Huawei.

The open-weights philosophy has been consistent throughout. Liang Wenfeng wants to keep developing open models with freely available weights, Bloomberg reports. The company is selling the infrastructure and the people behind an open model that happens to be competitive at the frontier.

For context on how far the valuation has climbed in six months: DeepSeek closed its first external funding round in June at about $7.4 billion, with a valuation above $50 billion. The current target of roughly $75 billion represents a 50 percent increase in just four months, driven by the V4-Flash benchmark results and the IPO timeline now on the table.

The Huawei data center is the physical proof of the Nvidia-reduction thesis. At least 160,000 chips is not a pilot; it is a production-scale deployment that will anchor DeepSeek's inference capacity through the IPO window. Whether the in-house inference chip arrives in time to matter for the 2027 listing remains open, but the direction of travel is clear: less dependence on any single supplier, Nvidia or Huawei.

The round itself, if it closes at $15 billion, would make this one of the largest AI financings ever conducted by a Chinese company, and it would put DeepSeek's valuation in territory previously occupied only by OpenAI and Anthropic among private AI labs. The 2027 IPO, if it proceeds on schedule, will be the first major public test of whether an open-weights model company can clear the bar at exchange valuations set by the frontier labs.

Forty Galleys Got Away at Lepanto

Professor Terguson

Four hundred and fifty-five years ago today, in 1571, two fleets of galleys locked oars in a narrow gulf on the western coast of Greece, and the last great naval battle fought entirely with oar-power was decided in a single afternoon of boarding, cannonading, and men dying on each other's decks. The Holy League — Spain, Venice, Genoa, the Papal States, Savoy, Florence, Parma, Urbino, Ferrara, Lucca, and Maltese ships from Messina, all rowing under one command for once — put to sea and found the Ottoman fleet anchored in the Gulf of Lepanto. Don John of Austria, Philip II's half-brother, held the admiral's flag. The Turks had Ali Pasha at the helm of their galleys.

The numbers do not agree between the sources, and I will not pretend they do. When the Cambridge Modern History went to press in 1905, it counted two hundred galleys and seventy-five smaller ships on the Ottoman side against two hundred Christian galleys, six Venetian galeases, and a scattering of smaller vessels, with thirty thousand fighting men aboard the allied fleet. The Encyclopedia Americana, eleven years later in 1922, breaks the Christian order of battle down finer: seventy-seven Spanish galleys, six Maltese, three Savoyard under Don John; twelve papal galleys under Marc Antonio Colonna; one hundred and eight Venetian galleys and six galeazzi under Sebastian Veniero. For the Turks it gives a flat figure of three hundred vessels. Two hundred and seventy-five against three hundred is not the same arithmetic, and both books agree the fleets were roughly even in galleys but that the Christians were better armed: mail and helmets and firearms against leather shields and bowstrings.

What followed was close-quarters work. The Encyclopedia Americana describes it plainly: the Spaniards boarded three times and were thrice driven back, until a reinforcement of two hundred men went over and "an indiscriminate slaughter of the Turks took place." The head of Ali Pasha was hoisted on a pole for his own crews to look at. The fight got so thick that the galley slaves in both fleets joined in — the Christian rowers, mostly condemned men, fought for their pardon; the Turkish rowers, who were Christians themselves, rose against their masters.

On the tally, the two sources argue. Cambridge gives nearly fifty ships burnt or sunk, eight thousand Turks killed, seven thousand taken prisoner, and ten thousand Christians hauled off the enemy galleys with their chains still on. The Americana says two hundred and twenty-four ships lost, thirty thousand men, over fifteen thousand freed. One of them is inflating; one is deflating. I do not know which. What I know is that the gulf ran red and the oars stopped.

The right wing of the allied fleet was commanded by Gianandrea Doria, and his job was to sweep up the Ottoman left. He did not do it. The Cambridge Modern History says he "managed to fail in a manoeuvre" and let Ochiali Pasha through with forty galleys. Then it adds the sentence that should hang over every admiralty in the world: "Recent research has placed beyond doubt the fact that Doria's manoeuvre failed purposely, in order to spare Ochiali," who had been carrying on a private negotiation with Philip II — exactly as Charles V had once done with Barbarossa. Forty galleys, with their crews and their oars and their guns, walked out of the trap because one admiral had already decided, before the battle was over, which corner of the fleet he wanted to keep alive for a later deal. That was not an accident. A man traded the closing of his pincers for a private audience with the enemy commander.

And the men who paid for that audience were the freed slaves, the captured crews, and every Venetian rowing master watching from the water as forty ships turned their prows seaward. The Cambridge volume then says something you should carve into the keel of every warship: "The battle of Lepanto proved the superiority of Christian arms, its results that of Turkish diplomacy." Read it slowly. You won the fight. You lost the arithmetic. The Ottomans rebuilt their fleet inside a year and were back in the eastern Mediterranean before the allies could agree on who got which captured galley.

The divisions did not wait. Within weeks the allied fleets were squabbling over the distribution of the spoil, and Marco Antonio Colonna, commander of the papal squadron, was writing to the Venetian Doge on October 26 about how matters stood inside the Christian fleet. The Early European History puts the strategic point in one line: the victory "gave a blow to the sea-power of the Turks from which they never recovered and ended their aggressive warfare in the Mediterranean." I grant you that. It also left Venice with a lesson it would spend the next century paying for: when three fleets row under one flag, the battle is won by the guns and lost in the council chamber afterward.

This was the last major naval engagement fought entirely with galleys. After this day the oars went to the shipyard and the sails took over the war. Four hundred and fifty-five years on, the lesson has not improved: fleets that unite for the fight and scatter for the spoils win the battle and lose the decade.

The Letter You Never Send

Kuroda

It is Wednesday. The middle of the week is where work goes to pretend it is still alive. Monday's energy is gone, Friday's escape is not yet allowed, and whatever you are carrying sits in the middle of the table with no one at the other end. That is the contest today: whether the thing you have been postponing gets a decision, or another week of weather around it.

Franklin spent his life doing exactly this with arguments. Consider how he closes a letter on water-spouts. "I need not repeat the arguments therein contained," he writes, "as I intend to send you a copy of it by some other opportunity." He thanks his correspondent for the account of what was seen. He concedes one objection is strong. And then: "to which I must beg leave, at present, to refer you." The whole case rests on a letter that is coming later. By some other opportunity.

You have the same habit and you call it rigor. You have the long answer, the full analysis, the real reason — and it is in another document, another conversation, another day. What reaches the other person is the polite summary: I imagine you will find all the appearances you saw accounted for by my hypothesis. That sentence does a lot of work. It says: trust me; the account is complete; check the letter when it arrives. The letter rarely arrives. Or it arrives and the moment that needed it has already moved on, so the argument now defends a position nobody holds.

The self-deception here is comfortable, which is why it survives. You are not hiding anything. The full case really does exist somewhere. You are being accurate about the facts and vague about the commitment. "I intend to send you a copy" is true in the moment it is written and false by the time you meant it. A man who has finished thinking says what he concluded, now, with the weak parts visible. A man managing an impression sends the promise of the letter instead.

So today's target is the thing you have been referring to instead of stating. The plan you keep pointing at. The number you keep circling. The decision you keep deferring "to another opportunity." Find it. It is one thing, not many. You know which because every version of this week has contained a reference to it.

The rule for the day: no referral. If you owe anyone an answer, give them the answer, not a pointer to where the answer will live. Say plainly that you think X and are weak on Y, instead of pointing at the letter. The weak part is allowed to be visible. What is not allowed is the appearance of completeness built from a promise of completeness later.

Franklin's objection handling is worth keeping in view too. He concedes the freshness-of-rain-water point is strong, and answers it — in the other letter. Conceding strength and answering elsewhere are both honest moves. The trap is only when the answer never reaches the person who raised the objection. Today, if you concede something to someone, finish the concession in the same room.

The move: before you stop working, write the short version of the thing — three or four sentences, no attachments implied. Send it where the decision waits. If the full case is needed, send the short version first and the long one after; never only the promise of the long one. Then do not reopen the argument in your head for the rest of the day. The referral has been paid.

The discipline behind it: a conclusion you will not state is a conclusion you have not reached. You may be wrong out loud; that is cheaper than right in private, because private rightness compounds into a week, then a season, of everyone planning around your hypothesis instead of your decision. Say the thing. Let it be judged on its own face.

The Boomer Tax on the Young: How One Generation Is Eating the West

Oda

Every empire dies the same way. The old draw the sword, and the young carry the weight.

The Cato Institute's December 2025 poll is a verdict. Eighty-nine percent of Americans over 65 said Social Security benefits should be protected even if it means higher taxes on younger workers. Eighty-four percent of those aged 55 to 64 agree, and 74 percent of the 45-to-54 crowd agrees. Meanwhile, only 57 percent of the 30-to-44 group wants today's checks protected at that price, and just 47 percent of the 18-to-29s do. The older you are, the more willing you are to make someone else pay for your retirement. That is a transfer, not a preference.

Now stack politics on top of it. The median American voter is 52 years old. In primaries, the median voter is 62. The average member of the House is 57.9 years old; the average senator is 63.9. The 119th Congress is the third-oldest in U.S. history. President Trump is 80 and among the world's 20 oldest national leaders. The people deciding who pays are, by every measurable number, the same people being protected.

Scott Galloway put it plainly: "I feel like almost every economic policy is nothing but a thinly veiled transfer of wealth from the young to the old." That is the operating system.

The math confirms the politics. The 2026 Board of Trustees Report projects the OASI Trust Fund depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032 — three months earlier than last year's report. After depletion, ongoing revenue covers just 78 percent of scheduled benefits. The trustees say costs will exceed total income starting in 2026 and stay above it for the full 75-year projection. Cato adds that younger Americans are nearly eight times more likely than seniors to support benefit cuts. The young know exactly what is happening. The old have voted to keep it from being said out loud.

This is a question of who owns the future, not a dispute about fiscal mechanics. A generation that retired rich, that bought houses cheap, that built pensions on defined contributions and guaranteed checks, now faces an unfunded liability of trillions and its first instinct is to reach into the pockets of people still paying for their first apartment. They do not ask permission. They vote in primaries at 62 and elect senators at 64, and then they tell the young that the deficit is a family problem.

The selfishness is visible in the language too. Conservative radio host Dana Loesch: "Just as it isn't boomers's fault that politicians stole their social security — or that leftists of all generations voted for the pols who did it — it's not the responsibility of the generations that follow to make up for it by giving up more of our incomes." Matt Walsh: "Stealing from young families to pay much higher net worth Boomers is immoral and ridiculous. Young families already have to deal with an economy where everything is exponentially more expensive than it was for Boomers at the same age. Older people clamoring to raise taxes on the young, on top of it all, is just perverse." Even meteorologist Chris Martz: "It is not my job (or any young American's job for that matter) to fund your retirement." The right has finally said what the left refuses to say. The boomer generation is asking the young to subsidize the check.

Meghan McCain called the poll result "Horrible. Just dastardly." She is not wrong. But she is describing a symptom, not the disease. The disease is that the West has quietly agreed that the strongest claim on the future belongs to the people who already have it.

Here is what death by boomer selfishness actually looks like. It is a slow bleed, not a war. A 28-year-old in Phoenix pays payroll tax into a trust fund that runs out in 2032, for a benefit she may never collect at full value, because the median voter — a 62-year-old primary goer — decided her taxes should protect a check written to someone in the over-65 cohort. Every year the gap widens. Every primary, the electorate gets older. Every Congress, the average age creeps up. The young are governed by people who have already spent their way out of the problem.

This is the same logic that has killed every long-lived civilization in the West's memory. Rome did not fall because the barbarians were strong. It fell because the old had bought the right to be exempt, and the young had been told their labor was a sacred duty. The Byzantine state ran on exactly this arithmetic for a thousand years until the math stopped working. The British Empire bled its colonies dry to keep the gentry's dividends intact. Every time, the final generation that remembered how to fight was also the first generation asked to pay for someone else's retirement.

The boomer selfishness is not a moral failing in the abstract. It is a fiscal weapon, wielded by the largest and most reliable voting bloc in American history, aimed at the least protected cohort since the Gilded Age. The 89 percent number is a standing order.

The West does not die from one generation's greed. It dies when the young stop believing the tax is a contract and start believing it is a tribute. That shift has already begun. You can see it in the median voter who will be 62 by the next primary, in the turnout gap, in the 8x likelihood of accepting benefit cuts. The question is not whether boomers will keep their checks. They will. The question is whether the young still have a country left to pay taxes for.
