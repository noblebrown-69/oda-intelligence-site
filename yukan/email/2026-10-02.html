DeepSeek and Huawei Open-Source Ascend Tools to Challenge Nvidia

Stark

DeepSeek has released open-source programming tools built with Huawei for the Chinese company's Ascend AI chips, in a push to cut reliance on Nvidia's software and hardware ecosystem. According to a September 30 Reuters report cited by Tom's Hardware, the release includes open-source libraries for AI computation and chip-to-chip communication, plus Ascend support for the high-level programming language TileLang.

The move targets the single biggest moat in AI compute: not the silicon itself, but the software stack that makes it usable. Nvidia's CUDA platform has long supplied developers with a mature programming environment and libraries tuned to its GPUs, and that ecosystem is a major part of the company's advantage in AI computing. By open-sourcing the Ascend side, DeepSeek and Huawei are trying to make the alternative path less painful for the developers who would have to migrate.

The released libraries break into two jobs. DeepGEMM-Ascend handles matrix multiplication and other calculations used in DeepSeek's models. It supports BF16, FP8, and FP4 operations and uses the same programming interfaces as DeepSeek's existing DeepGEMM library, so developers can keep familiar APIs when they move to Ascend. DeepEP-Ascend handles communication for training and inference, including routing data to the different experts in mixture-of-experts models and combining their outputs. Both libraries were developed and tested on Ascend 950 hardware, Tom's Hardware reported.

TileLang sits on top as the higher-level programming layer for writing optimized kernels. DeepSeek described it as offering "a simpler programming model" than Nvidia's CUDA, with the aim of improving development efficiency and simplifying code. The language already supported Nvidia and other hardware, with earlier adapters available for Huawei's Ascend processors. The September 30 update adds native support for Ascend 950, including code generation, automatic scheduling, and synchronization.

The two companies also worked together to optimize computation and communication on a supernode system built around 128 Ascend 950 chips. That work addresses the two requirements for running large AI workloads across multiple accelerators: performing calculations efficiently on each chip, and moving data quickly enough between them to keep the processors occupied. DeepSeek says the tools, for which Huawei provided full support during development, are intended to simplify programming while letting developers fully leverage the hardware's performance.

The timing matters. The announcement came two weeks after Huawei unveiled its next generation of AI processors and supernode systems, with Huawei saying it expected its AI systems to be widely used for model training in 2027. The companies had already collaborated on DeepSeek's V4 model, released in preview form in April, with support for Huawei's Ascend chips. Huawei said its Ascend 950 supernodes fully supported the V4 models and that its chips had been used for part of the lighter V4-Flash model's training.

For developers already deep in CUDA, the practical pitch is incremental rather than a rip-and-replace: keep the DeepGEMM interface you know, add a communication library matched to your mixture-of-experts layout, and write kernels in a language that claims a simpler model while still supporting Nvidia GPUs. Whether that lowers the switching cost enough to matter at scale is what the next year of supernode deployments will test.

The King-Maker Throws the King Off

Professor Terguson

The fashionable story of this year is a tidy little tale about a king. It says that in 1470 Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, rebelled against Edward IV of England, drove him across the water into Holland, and put Henry VI back on the throne. Clean. Neat. A duke who got cross at his master and showed him the door.

That is not a rebellion. That is a man who made the king losing his own crown.

Here is what actually happened, and it is bloodier and uglier than the tidy version. The War of the Roses had been running since 1455. Warwick was not some obscure cousin who stumbled into a grudge. He won at Northampton in July 1460 and took Henry VI prisoner; he reentered London with Edward in 1461; he won the victory of Towton in 1461, the bloody defeat that put the Yorkist line on the throne. The man had built this kingdom's crown with his own hands. He was made warden of the Scottish marches, constable of Dover, lord high chamberlain. In plain terms: he was more powerful than the king who wore the hat.

And then Edward did what kings do. He married Elizabeth Woodville against Warwick's will and leaned on Burgundy instead of his own chief duke. So Warwick drove into revolt, took the king prisoner in 1469, and released him again, a man holding a knife to your throat while he decides whether he likes your face. In 1470 he conspired with his son-in-law Clarence, fled to France, adopted the Lancastrian cause, landed in England, drove Edward IV to Flanders, and restored Henry VI in 1470.

Read that last line slowly. The king's own best general crossed the sea with a foreign army, marched on London, and installed an imbecile on the throne in his place. The New International Encyclopaedia says Henry, although an imbecile, was reinstated as King. That is a coup wearing a coronet.

Now here is where the popular story goes soft, and I will not let it. It makes Warwick the villain and Edward the wronged king. Wrong twice over. Warwick was the power behind every Yorkist victory from 1460 to 1464, and when he turned, the whole house went down with him. He did not merely beat Edward; he made the man a refugee in Holland for a year. That is what a king-maker does when the king stops making him comfortable.

And then there is the ending, which the tidy story skips because it is too funny to be true. Warwick's son-in-law Clarence had helped turn on Edward, and Warwick's own men would soon turn on Warwick. Edward IV returned in the early part of 1471, and on 14 April decisively defeated Warwick at Barnet, the king-maker himself falling in the fight. The man who made a king was killed by that king's return.

That is the lesson of 1470, and it is not about courtesy or succession law. It is about what happens when one man becomes more necessary to the crown than the man on the throne. You do not get to keep the crown and the man who made it both alive. One of them eats the other.

The encyclopedia line that gets it is blunt enough: he drove Edward IV to Flanders, and restored Henry VI in 1470. Two verbs. No ceremony. That is how a real coup reads when somebody finally writes it down without making it pretty.

Stop Forgiving Everything

Kuroda

It is Friday. The week is spent. Whatever you did or failed to do has consequences that are now fixed; the only open question is what they mean and what you do next. That makes today a day for judgment, not production. The contest you are actually in is with your own ledger: which of this week's moves were real, which were performance, and where you stored power instead of spending it.

Nietzsche writes in The Antichrist: "This tolerance and largeur of the heart that 'forgives' everything because it 'understands' everything is a sirocco to us." That sentence is about fear wearing a friendly face. The modern habit is to understand the person who disappointed you, the market that moved against you, the version of yourself that talked too much or stayed silent. Understanding costs nothing. Forgetting what the thing cost is how comfort eats your judgment from inside.

The man who forgives everything is afraid to price the world. If every bad act gets absorbed by understanding, then no one pays anything for it — including you. You stop noticing where the pressure is coming from. Nietzsche says he and his kind "thirsted for the lightnings and great deeds" and kept away "from the happiness of the weakling, from 'resignation'." The point is this: a life without a goal turns endurance into weather. You just stand in it until something else happens to you.

Here is what Friday is for. Look at the week the way a surveyor looks at ground. The job is to find out where it holds. Three questions, answered in writing if you can manage that:

One: What did I actually win this week? Win means position gained — a commitment secured, a number closed, a no that was finally said and held. Getting through it does not count. If the list is short, that is data, not shame. Short lists are how you find out your effort went into terrain that doesn't keep.

Two: What did I forgive that I should have charged? The late delivery, the soft yes, the meeting where everyone agreed to disagree forever. Naming it is bookkeeping. You are deciding what the price of this relationship or arrangement is, and whether you can afford it next quarter.

Three: Where did I store power instead of spending it? Saved energy, a dry reserve, a no-you-don't-need-to-know that cost nothing. Stored power is the only kind that compounds. Spent power is just motion.

The hard constraint today: one goal. Nietzsche's formula for his people was "a Yea, a Nay, a straight line, a goal." You get one for the coming week. Not three priorities. One. The test is simple — if you cannot say what Monday looks like when it has been won, what you have is a mood. A goal names its own victory condition and its own failure state. Write both down. The failure state matters more: most people only define success, which means every ambiguous Tuesday gets quietly classified as fine.

What to attack today is the soft places. The arrangement you keep because it understands you. The commitment you keep because it's familiar. The excuse that has started sounding like a plan. You do not have to kill them all. Pick the one that costs the most per week and either charge it properly or end it. That single move does more for your next three months than any productivity system, because it changes what other people expect of you, and expectations are the cheapest force multiplier there is.

The discipline: at the end of this Friday's work, before the weekend starts, say the goal out loud in one sentence, with its failure state attached. Then do not argue with yourself about it again until Monday. The man who renegotiates his own target every morning is paying a toll to his own appetite and calling it strategy.

Live amid the ice if you have to. But pick the place first.
