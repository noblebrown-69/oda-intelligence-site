Starship Cadence Cut Sparks Short Seller Challenge

SpaceX ended its first full quarter as a public company in the red. The stock closed Wednesday at $150.86, up 1.1%, after falling 12% in the third quarter despite gaining 5% in September. Jim Chanos, founder of Kynikos Associates and known for predicting Enron's 2001 collapse, challenged the company's launch outlook after satellite communications consultant Tim Farrar highlighted a shift in Elon Musk's targets. On an Aug. 4 earnings call, Musk said that "probably a year from now, we will be doing at least one flight a day, possibly more." In an interview this week alongside Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, Musk offered a different target: "We deployed V3 Starlink satellites, which are enormous. These are satellites with a wingspan of a 737. Next year, we expect Starship to reach a weekly or twice weekly launch cadence," he said. Aaron Burnett, founder and CEO of Mach33, interpreted that as "50-100 launches annualized in 2027." Chanos called it an 80% cut in forecasted 2027 Starship launches by Musk, comparing 75 launches with 365 at a sustained daily rate. Farrar countered that the revised cadence could represent a year-end objective rather than a sustained launch rate throughout 2027. Stocktwits

Musk and Delta CEO Trade Barbs Over Starlink Rejection

Delta's decision to use Amazon's Leo WiFi instead of Starlink has sparked a public clash between Elon Musk and CEO Ed Bastian. Delta is the lone major U.S. carrier choosing Amazon (AMZN) Leo-powered WiFi, a rival to Starlink, the fastest in-flight internet available today. Musk took aim at Delta's connectivity, posting on X that the "best way to sandbox an AI is to put it on a Delta flight – it will have no chance of accessing the Internet!" The jab followed an earlier post in which Musk said Delta "will lose a lot of customers." Bastian answered to Musk's jibe: "We do not want to be with Elon Musk. Trust me," stocktwits reported. At an earlier event, Bastian said Delta did test Starlink but chose not to partner with Musk: "If you know his reputation, it's all true what they say about him," he said. After reports of those comments, Musk said the CEO "will lose his job." Delta Air Lines stock edged 0.3% higher in Thursday's premarket and has declined 1.7% so far this week. Stocktwits

Cathie Wood Backs Tesla-SpaceX Merger, Sees 'Working All Angles' With Trump and Xi

SEC Sues Fund Manager Over Pre-IPO SpaceX and OpenAI Shares, Alleging Strip Club Spending

The Securities and Exchange Commission is dropping the hammer on private fund advisers who allegedly mishandled millions in investor assets under the guise of granting them lucrative pre-IPO shares in coveted startups such as OpenAI, SpaceX, and others. According to two separate cases announced Wednesday, the SEC claims multiple fund advisers allegedly deceived mom-and-pop investors—including Navy veterans—about where the millions they thought they invested actually went. In one case, the SEC sued Owen Meyer, 35, and his firm, Meyer Global Management in federal Court in Manhattan, alleging Meyer raised at least $18.5 million from nearly 100 investors while misappropriating at least $1.27 million in client money along the way. The SEC claims Meyer spent more than $18,000 in fund capital for his "personal entertainment" at a strip club one night in April 2023 that spilled into the wee hours of the morning. Meyer allegedly tried to pay a $4,400 bill to the club at 4:41 a.m. using a debit card associated with Meyer Global Partners, but it was declined twice, the SEC claims. Just minutes later, Meyer transferred $10,000 from a fund account containing only investor money to the Meyer Global Partners account. He then allegedly paid the club $4,400 at 4:44 a.m. and then another $3,650 at 5:30 a.m. for receipts that listed drinks, "entertainment room rental fees," and included the name of Meyer's cocktail server at the club, the SEC claims. That same night, Meyer allegedly transferred $10,000 directly from the same fund account, which held investor money raised to buy shares of online casino operator Playstar. He transferred the money to the manager of the strip club, the SEC alleges. Memo lines on the payments listed "movie tickets and theatre performance," and "opera." Yahoo Finance

The potato is not simple. Neither are you.

Kuroda

Thursday. The week's spine. Monday's noise has settled, Friday's relief is still a rumor, and whatever you have been avoiding all week is now standing in the middle of the room looking at you. This is the day when the shape of your position shows. You can work it or you can perform working on it. One of those ends well.

The theme today is deception, and not the kind you do to other people. The kind done to you, by things that look simple.

Spencer looks at a potato. "Cut it through and observe how structureless is its substance." To the naked eye, a lump. Uniform, quiet, one thing. Then he puts a lens on it. The mass is "permeated by vessels of complex formation." It is made of innumerable cells, each with layered walls. Each cell holds starch grains, and each grain is "formed of layer within layer, like the coats of an onion." Where there appears perfect simplicity there is really complexity within complexity.

That is your week right now, if you are honest about it. The project that looks like one task is a stack of tasks, each with its own wall, each holding smaller grains. The person who looks easy to deal with runs on fear, interest, and honor in an order you have never checked. The market that looks flat has structure underneath it that will decide the price before you are finished deciding what to do.

The deception is a surface report your mind accepts as the whole file. The first glance tells the truth about surface and nothing else. You accept it anyway.

Here is the move. When something looks simple this week, do not believe it and do not immediately distrust it either. Cut it. One cut. Ask what is inside before you decide whether to eat it, sell it, or throw it at someone. A day of cutting beats a month of being surprised by things that "looked straightforward."

One hard constraint: one cut per problem per day. Not ten. Ten cuts on the same potato is not diligence; it is anxiety with a knife. Pick the single most load-bearing thing in your week — the task whose failure would make everything else irrelevant — and find out what its layers are. Then stop cutting and act on what you found.

The discipline: write down one thing that looked simple this morning, and one fact you learned about it by afternoon. If the list is empty at night, you spent the day performing work. If it has an entry, you spent the day seeing.

After the move, hold the citadel. You still control what you cut open. What you have not cut yet is theirs — or worse, its own — until you look.

Thursday does not forgive men who mistake a surface for a position and build on it. Cut once. See the onion. Act.

The Plan to Rebuild the West: Seven Commands, Not a Eulogy

Oda

The diagnosis is not in dispute. Birth rates at record lows, a middle class that can no longer afford the house, the farm, or the factory floor it used to own, immigration streams that outpace the states' capacity to absorb them, universities teaching softness as virtue, and an industry base that outsourced its spine to rivals. I will not spend this column on the wound. I will spend it on the knife.

The plan is seven commands. A platform and a think-tank report give options; a civilization in retreat needs orders.

Command One: The family is national policy, not private preference. The United States recorded 3,606,400 births in 2025, a one percent decline from 2024, and the general fertility rate fell to 53.1 per 1,000 women aged 15 to 44 — the twenty-fifth year of a slide that began in 2007. The remedy is arithmetic before sentiment: child tax credits large enough that a family of five lives better on one income than a childless couple does on two; housing codes that let you build what your grandfather built; and immigration ordered to the size of the labor force actually present, not the size of the lobby in the capital. A nation that cannot replace itself is a nation with a scheduled end date.

Command Two: Make the state small enough to be trusted. The 10-year Treasury yield climbed to 5.14% on September 24 as markets priced in strong business data and tighter Federal Reserve policy — proof that the bond market still holds the leash Congress has dropped. Every dollar the federal government spends is a dollar not spent by a family, a shop, or a parish. The plan: cap the deficit at two percent of GDP for a decade, then let states keep what their citizens earn, and cut every agency that cannot explain its existence in one sentence to a layman.

Command Three: Teach like the stakes are real. A boy who leaves school able to recite but unable to build, write, or command is a liability, not an asset. The plan: mastery before promotion in the first eight years; reading, writing, arithmetic, history, and one trade every student learns with his hands by sixteen; and teachers given the standing to demand effort from both students and parents. We do not need more schools. We need schools that mean it.

Command Four: Build where you can build. The chip market tells the whole story in one afternoon. On September 24, Lam Research fell 2.2%, Marvell Technology 2%, Western Digital 4.1%, and FormFactor 3.9% as yields rose — but the deeper problem is not that day's tape. It is that we debate tariffs on advanced semiconductors in a summit hall while our fabs, our rare earths, and our memory all sit across the Pacific. The plan: domestic production of anything strategic — chips, batteries, steel, medicine, grain — with subsidies paid for by ending the subsidies on things we do not need.

Command Five: Trade like you intend to win. President Trump and President Xi Jinping met in Washington and extended their tariff truce two months, to January 10, but left rare earths unresolved — no substantive pact on shipments or export licensing. A truce is not a settlement. The plan: keep the truce where it works, hold the line where it does not, and build the rare-earth and semiconductor supply chains at home so that the next summit is a choice, not a necessity.

Command Six: Fund the sword. The Department of Defense's FY2026 budget request stands at $961.6 billion — a 13.4 percent increase over FY2025 — and its own words are the plan: "rebuild America's military, reestablish deterrence, and restore the warrior ethos." That is the right doctrine. The order now is to spend it on warfighting platforms and people, not on the machinery of its own administration, and to keep spending until the adversary stops testing.

Command Seven: Let the churches ring again. Faith is not decoration on a civilization; it is the mortar between the bricks. Sunday mass, Friday night football, and the local bank are the three institutions that actually hold a town together. The plan: tax code that rewards the family and the church, zoning that lets a congregation stand where a parking lot sits, and public life that does not mock what its citizens believe when the cameras go off.

None of this is soft, and none of it is new. Nobunaga unified Japan with fewer commands than these; Ieyasu finished the job by refusing to die on principle. The West has the same seven problems in the same order it always has: too many children unafforded, too much state in the middle, schools that do not mean it, factories abroad, treaties without teeth, a sword underfunded, and a God who is only invited for the wedding.

The plan is not hard to see. It is hard to execute, which is why it has been waiting.
