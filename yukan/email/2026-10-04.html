US Arrests CEO Over $300M Nvidia Chip Smuggling Ring

Stark

The Department of Justice arrested Greg Lui, 38, chief executive of Earthmade Computer, over a smuggling operation that funneled more than $300 million in export-controlled Nvidia chips into China. In a press release on Thursday, the DOJ accused Lui, also known as Yiu Kong Lui, of using false paperwork to mask shipments of servers he allegedly knew were bound for China.

The FBI's indictment alleged that Lui conspired with freight-forwarding firms in South Asian countries including Malaysia and Singapore to divert the chips, which included Nvidia A100 and H100 graphics processors. Those are not Nvidia's most advanced parts, but they can process large amounts of data and train large language models, which the US worries could pose national security risks if China gains enough of them to advance its AI or strengthen its military.

Emails and bank records exposed the scheme, the DOJ alleged, which ran from October 2023 until August 2026. One key exhibit is a 2024 email discussing an order for 70 servers with export-restricted GPUs that were fraudulently claimed as destined for Malaysia. After "Lui submitted a purchase order to a US manufacturer for 27 of these servers for approximately $7,614,000," the DOJ alleged that a co-conspirator told a Malaysian government official that all 27 were shipped to China. A similar shipment of 92 export-controlled servers was allegedly routed through Singapore to Malaysia and then Hong Kong, before reaching a Chinese firm based in Hangzhou, which The Wall Street Journal last year described as China's AI hub.

Another flagged 2024 shipment shows how far Lui allegedly went to fool US officials. When shipping 100 Malaysian-bound servers each containing Nvidia H100 GPUs worth over $22 million, Lui allegedly submitted fraudulent documentation from a fake buyer identifying its chief executive as "Jackie Lui." Three years earlier, the FBI alleged, he had purchased the identifying documents of an individual whose identity is known to the grand jury and used that identity to conduct business in furtherance of the export evasion scheme.

In 2024, Lui's firm allegedly received more than $176 million from the operation. Payment records from his accounts at Bank of America and JP Morgan provided further evidence, the DOJ said. Arguing the evidence backs the charges, the department wants to seize all funds gained through the scheme. Arrested on three counts, Lui is accused of conspiring to violate the Export Control Reform Act and the Export Administration Regulations, as well as federal smuggling and money laundering laws. He faces up to 20 years in prison if found guilty of either the conspiracy or money laundering counts, and the smuggling charge carries a maximum of up to 10 years.

The arrest lands in the middle of a wider reckoning for Nvidia. Lui's case follows an Nvidia senior manager based in Taiwan who was linked to a similar scheme allegedly run by ex-Supermicro staff. At the time, Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang urged Supermicro to fix its compliance problem while claiming there was no evidence of smuggling. Since then, as more arrests have followed, Nvidia has shifted from denying smuggling to downplaying the role smuggled chips play in China's AI industry, which is now largely powered by Huawei chips. On Thursday, a spokesperson told Bloomberg that "less than one half of one percent to Nvidia products" have allegedly been diverted to China.

Bloomberg's investigation, "based on conversations with dozens of people across five countries," suggested the black market is "hard to measure" and likely much bigger than reported. Officials around the world suspect hundreds of thousands of AI chips are being exchanged in a sprawling shadow trade that may power small data centers and potentially even "a handful of hyperscalers" in China. Those chips could fill a hardware gap China does not expect to close on its own until 2030, one "top executive" told Bloomberg.

Nvidia disagrees that it could have stopped the shipments officials have flagged. A spokesperson told Bloomberg that "a startup's growth plan in a friendly nation is an opportunity for America, not a 'red flag' to be feared." The company will keep working with Megaspeed, an entity the US has recently flagged as potentially evading curbs, until it is ordered to stop.

"Government officials and industry experts are increasingly pointing to gaps in Nvidia's due diligence, questioning how the company failed to catch red flags," Bloomberg reported.

Starship Flight 14 Puts SpaceX in Orbit — and on a Revenue Path

SpaceX's Starship completed its first non-demonstration, revenue-generating launch with Flight 14 on Monday, Sept. 28. Super Heavy made a water landing seven minutes in, and mission control cleared the spacecraft to relight one engine at the 18-minute-20-second mark. The flight deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites before SpaceX cut it short after one orbit, landing in the Pacific three hours and eight minutes after launch. It stands 407 feet tall and carries more than 100 metric tons to low Earth orbit, roughly four times Falcon 9's payload. The company has reportedly stopped taking Falcon 9 orders after 2028; Starship will replace it. The Motley Fool

NASA Turns to Boeing's Starliner as SpaceX Retires Falcon and Dragon

With SpaceX concentrating on Starship, NASA is contracting Boeing to fly the troubled Starliner spacecraft at least five more times — potentially seven. Administrator Jared Isaacman said "SpaceX intends to sunset older platforms like Falcon and Dragon," leaving a gap in Low Earth Orbit access that Starliner will fill. The first flight, an uncrewed test, is expected December 2026 or January 2027, followed by an 18-month pause and a crewed ISS mission in mid-2028. NASA Low Earth Orbit Program manager Dana Weigel said, "Starliner is what we're intending to fly into the future." The contract carries $359 million in funding. The Motley Fool

The Day the Calendar Lied and the Church Fixed It

Professor Terguson

Four hundred forty-four years ago this month, a pope deleted ten days from the year. That is not a metaphor. That is not a rounding error in some ledger nobody audits. In March of 1582, Pope Gregory XIII issued a brief abolishing the Julian calendar in all Catholic countries and substituting what we still call the new style. And the amendment it ordered was this: ten days were to be dropped after 4 October 1582, and the 15th was reckoned immediately after the 4th. So in Rome a Wednesday, 4 October, was followed by a Thursday, the 15th. Nobody died of that. Everybody noticed. The popular story you'll hear is that the calendar drifted a little and some scholar tidied it up. That is the sanitized version. Here is what actually happened, and why it matters to anyone who has ever trusted a date.

The Julian year was wrong. Not by much, not in one year — but enough, and forever compounding. The received length of 365 and a quarter days exceeds the true tropical year by about eleven minutes, so that for every such Julian year the equinox receded eleven minutes, or a day in about 130 years. Do you hear that? A slow leak. You cannot feel it in your own lifetime, which is precisely why it was dangerous. By the sixteenth century the vernal equinox had changed its place in the calendar from the 21st to the 10th; it really took place on the 10th instead of the 21st on which it was placed in the calendar. The date the Church set for the spring feast was now sitting on top of a sun that had moved out from under it. That is not bookkeeping. That is the whole liturgical year slowly sliding off its anchor, and nobody with a pulse can watch that for two centuries without someone reaching for the knife.

Now the man who built the fix was dead before he saw it work. The author of the system adopted by Gregory was Aloysius Lilius, or Luigi Lilio Ghiraldi, a learned astronomer and physician of Naples, who died, however, before its introduction. He projected a plan for amending the calendar, which after his death was presented by his brother to Pope Gregory XIII. The pope then did what popes do when they smell a chance at glory: he perceiving that the measure was likely to confer a great éclat on his pontificate, undertook the long-desired reformation. And having found the governments of the principal Catholic states ready to adopt his views, he issued the brief in March 1582. Note the machinery. This was not one man in a tower doing arithmetic. The pope assembled a number of prelates and learned men; in 1577 the proposed change was adopted by all the Catholic princes. A calendar reform is a treaty before it is a math problem. You cannot unilaterally delete ten days from everyone's wedding anniversaries without the governments agreeing to bleed you.

And here is the part the popular story loves to skip, because it is inconvenient. The brief only bound the countries that took it in. In 1582 Gregory issued the reform and introduced in its stead the one now in use, under the name of the Gregorian or reformed calendar, or the new style, as the other was now called the old style. But the old style did not die. It went into hiding, and it kept living. The mode of correcting the calendar has been adopted at different times by almost all civilized nations except Russia and other countries where the Greek Church is predominant, which still adhere to the Old Style. England itself was a holdout; the Gregorian or New Style was adopted there by act of Parliament only later, not in 1582. So for a century after Gregory's brief, two calendars ran side by side across Europe, and any date written down had to carry a suffix — O.S. or N.S. — or else it was a lie about which year you meant. The reformed calendar and the old one were two clocks, not two versions of the same clock, and the gap between them was political, religious, and stubbornly alive.

The rule itself is worth learning because it is the cleanest piece of engineering in the whole mess. Every 100th year, which by the old style was a leap year, was now to be a common year. That single clause — drop the leap day on century years that don't divide evenly — is what keeps the Gregorian year locked to the sun to this hour. It is the difference between a calendar you have to trust and one you can check. The Julian rule was four years, every four years, no exceptions; simple, and wrong by eleven minutes a year. The Gregorian rule adds the century carve-out and buys back the drift. That is not elegance for its own sake. That is a man who read the ledger long enough to see the leak and patched it at the source instead of mopping the floor.

So what is the lesson, stripped of the seminar-speak? A system that is wrong by a little, every single time, is more dangerous than one that is wrong by a lot once in a while, because you never feel the small error until it has compounded into a decade. The Julian calendar was not abolished because anyone noticed eleven minutes. It was abolished because somebody did the arithmetic for thirteen centuries of equinoxes and found the spring feast had wandered off its own holiday. That is how most institutions actually fail: not in a crash, but in a drift so slow that each generation swears the ground is level under their feet. And the fix only held because it was backed by a pope, a brother who carried a dead man's plan to the throne, and every Catholic prince in Europe signing on before the ink dried.

The next time someone hands you a date without telling you which calendar it came from, ask. The answer is not pedantry. It is the difference between a clock that was corrected once, by men who knew exactly how far it had slipped, and one that quietly keeps losing eleven minutes a year while everybody celebrates on the wrong day.

The Crown Holds While the People Are Willing

Kuroda

Sunday is a low day. You have no audience to perform for until Monday. That makes it the best day of the week to look at what you actually hold and what you are pretending to hold.

Aristotle watched kingdoms fail and found a pattern that still holds in every office, partnership, and household: "Kingdoms are seldom destroyed by any outward attack; for which reason they are generally very stable; but they have many causes of subversion within." The enemy is inside the walls. The people who hold power with you are the ones who will take it from you, either by stirring a sedition or by quietly taking more than their share and calling it normal.

That is your terrain today. Not your rivals across the street. Your partners at the table.

Aristotle draws the line that matters: "a kingly government is one that is voluntarily submitted to." The moment submission stops being voluntary, you have a tyranny, and tyrannies do not last because every subject keeps a private ledger of what was taken without consent. You can see this in your own life. Count the times you agreed to a deal, a schedule, or a favor and then kept giving more than you bargained for. Every extra thing went on the ledger as involuntary. The other party stopped paying attention to whether you wanted it. That is how a kingly arrangement becomes a tyranny without a single dramatic scene.

The second point is about contempt. Aristotle says one cause of revolution in a hereditary kingdom is "that many of those who enjoy it are naturally proper objects of contempt only." People do not revolt because they are hungry. They revolt because the man in charge has become boring, careless, or small. You can afford to be lazy. You cannot afford to be contemptible. The difference is the whole game.

Here is what to attack today. Pick one relationship where you have been taking more than giving for a while — a team, a spouse, a business partner, a friendship — and write down the last three things you took without asking. Then decide what you will give back this week that costs you something real. Make it a concession with weight in it, not a gift.

Your one hard constraint: no new commitments today. Sunday is for reading your own position, not for expanding it. Every yes you give today while tired or unwatched becomes an involuntary thing someone else can later point to and say he already agreed.

The move: do the one concession before Monday. The discipline: keep a running account of what you take without asking, and settle it quarterly. A position held by consent is worth ten times one held by force, because it survives the day the force runs out.

White Male Privilege Is a Myth. The Deck Has Been Stacked Since 1961.

Oda

Ask the average American what "white male privilege" means and you get a picture of some man who never had to work for anything, handed a job by his surname, promoted past every woman in the building. That man is not walking around anymore. He was a casualty of the very system that now claims to mourn him.

Here is the timeline, and it is short enough to fit on one hand. On March 6, 1961, President John F. Kennedy signed Executive Order 10925. It required government contractors to take affirmative action so that applicants were treated equally regardless of race, color, religion, or national origin, and to stop discriminating in hiring, firing, or promotion. It was an order with teeth: lose the contract if you did not comply. Three years later, Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1964, and Title VII put employment discrimination on the federal books. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission followed in 1965 to enforce it all. From that point forward, the rules of the game changed. You could no longer hire a white man over a Black woman and call it a business decision. You had to justify every pass.

That is what "the deck stacked" means. It does not mean white men were punished with quotas. It means the default assumption flipped: the employer now bears the burden of proving he did not discriminate, and the applicant gets a government agency in his corner for free. Sixty-five years of that machinery is why a 2026 Arkansas welfare ad featuring a video-game-playing white man named "Brad" set off alarms across the conservative world.

This week, Governor Sarah Huckabee Sanders ran an ad promoting her new work requirements for welfare recipients. The villain was Brad, a tousle-headed young white male mashing a controller and scarfing potato chips. "Brad has turned relaxing into a full-time profession," Sanders says in the spot. "But you, you actually work hard. You pay taxes to cover welfare for guys like Brad who just choose not to work." It is good policy. Work requirements are a long-standing conservative goal. But the far right did not care about the policy. They cared that the face on the ad was white and male.

Matt Walsh of The Daily Wire called the number of single white men on welfare "minuscule" and "practically non-existent," and demanded Republicans have "the balls to do this ad with 'Shaniqua' as the villain." Pastor Dale Partridge said the ad "villainizes a white man." James Fishback called it "disgraceful anti-white propaganda." Auron MacIntyre wrote that the first party to stop "hating young white men" will win for a generation. Jack Posobiec asked Grok why it is "always a white male." A Republican governor, running a conservative policy, gets attacked by conservatives because the poster boy is a white man. That is not what a privileged class does. A privileged class does not riot when someone points at one of its own.

The data behind the ad actually supports Sanders. According to KFF's most recent analysis, 36% of Medicaid recipients were white, 24% Hispanic and 19% Black in 2023. The "Brad" caricature is the single largest category on that chart. What the far right found was a cultural nerve: the image of the lazy white male has become so sacred that depicting him offends more than the policy does.

Now step back to 1961 and ask who benefited from that flip. The Greatest Generation — the men who fought in World War II, built the factories, and filled the middle class — sold their grandchildren out for a war they did not start. They signed away the unspoken rule that a white man could be hired on merit alone and replaced with a system where every hiring decision is subject to review, appeal, and charge. The men who won the war got the suburbs; the men who lost it get the EEOC.

The numbers today are not flattering to the "privilege" story. In 2021, men received 42% of bachelor's degrees awarded in the United States, the lowest male share on record, down from 57% in 1970, according to an analysis published at aibm.org of National Center for Education Statistics data. White men's slice of the degree pie has been shrinking since at least the 1980s. In fiscal year 2024, the EEOC received 88,531 new discrimination charges — a 9.2% increase from the prior year — and ended the year with 52,080 still pending. The machine that enforces anti-discrimination law is accelerating.

None of this is an argument that white men are being crushed. It is an argument that the word "privilege" has outlived its subject. Privilege means you get something for nothing. White men today get a shrinking share of degrees, a labor market where every hire is a potential charge, and a cultural script in which they are the default villain in every diversity conversation. The word fails. It describes a class that was told it was the heir to the estate, watched the will get rewritten, and then got mad when the new heirs moved in.

The Greatest Generation made a deal with history. They won the war, built the country, and agreed — for the most part quietly — that the next generation would be judged by a different set of rules than the one they grew up under. The price was fairness on paper. The cost was the unspoken assumption that a white man's name on a resume would do the work. That assumption is gone. It has been gone since March 6, 1961.

The men who are still arguing about Brad on X are not defending privilege. They are mourning its loss. And there is no shame in mourning something you actually had. The shame is pretending it never left.
